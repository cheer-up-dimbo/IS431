1. Introduction (Zakir)

Boxing is widely recognized as a physically demanding and inherently dangerous sport. The World Medical Association has raised particular concern, stating that the “main medical argument against boxing is the risk of chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE)”, a degenerative brain condition linked to repeated head trauma. Beyond long-term neurological risks, acute injuries are also prevalent in competitive boxing (G., Magdalena, Lindsay, I., & M., 2019).

A comparative study analysing 7,712 athlete exposures across three consecutive Olympic Games emphasised the pressing need for “injury prevention initiatives to reduce the burden of injury among combat sport athletes.” Notably, the findings revealed that injury risk in boxing shows “no difference by sex, weight category, or tournament round,” accenting that the hazards of the sport affect athletes indiscriminately, regardless of demographic or competitive context (Lystad, Alevras, Rudy, Soligard, & Engebretsen, 2021).

Given these challenges, there is a growing necessity for solutions that mitigate injury risk during training. This is especially crucial for elite athletes, where sparring remains an indispensable component of preparation but simultaneously exposes them to the highest risk of harm. Therefore, alternative training methods are needed approaches that replicate the intensity and realism of sparring while offering a safer environment.

2. Problem Clarification (Zakir)

2.1 Background

Sparring is widely regarded as the cornerstone of boxing training, as it represents the “combat” element of the sport and enables athletes to put their techniques into practice under realistic conditions. It is during sparring training that boxers sharpen essential skills such as timing, defence, and adaptability under pressure. However, research indicates that “repetitive sub-concussive trauma from frequent and intense sparring training appears to be a significant risk for developing neurological sequelae”, highlighting the health risks associated with its practice (Stiller, et al., 2014). While sparring training remains indispensable, these risks point to the need for safer alternatives that can replicate its training benefits without compromising athlete well-being.

To better understand this problem, our team engaged directly with the boxing community by signing up for boxing gym memberships. We conducted user interviews, alongside a secondary survey of existing solutions and competitors in the boxing training landscape. From these findings, we developed two value proposition canvas to capture the needs and pain points of both boxers and the community.

2.2 Primary Research: User Interviews/Surveys

2.2.1 Methodology

Our research team conducted user interviews at six different boxing gyms in Singapore to broaden the scope of perspectives.

Our research method was guided by the Interaction Design Foundation (IDF) framework, where user interviews are carried out during the empathize phase of the design thinking process (IxDF, 2016). While at the various boxing gyms, we employed IDF’s method of Unstructured Interviews, enabling a “qualitative exploration of users’ thoughts and experiences” (IxDF, 2016). In addition, we also designed and sent out survey forms to get more viewpoints from boxers that we did not meet in-person from the aforementioned gyms. Through our user interview and surveys, we defined the different stakeholders in the boxing community into two groups;

- Boxing-Gym Boxers: People who train recreationally or competitively in boxing gyms are known as boxers.

- Training Support Stakeholders: Stakeholders in the training support ecosystem include parents, trainers, and gym owners who plan, coordinate, and encourage boxing involvement and progress.

Questions are tailored accordingly and refined them into a concise set of guiding questions (Table 1).

2.2.2 Insights

Our user interview and surveys of Boxing-Gym Boxers presented that sparring training still accounts for about 22.6% + 15.1% (≈37.7%) of reported difficulties highlights the fact that sparring training continues to be boxers' main source of both attraction and annoyance (Figure 1). Finding reliable partners who can match their ability and energy levels is a challenge for many boxers, which can result in lower-quality training or a higher risk of injury. However, about 20.8% of respondents say they would like to learn and advance more quickly but feel constrained by the format of the lessons that are offered. Because different skill levels are frequently mixed in group training, more experienced boxers are forced to hold back or repeat fundamental drills, which hinders their progress and saps their desire. Essentially, even while sparring training is essential for development, the way gyms are set up now frequently makes it difficult to customize the level of intensity and learning rate, which leaves experienced boxers constrained in development and those looking to improve constrained by inflexible class schedules.

Approximately 33% of coaches within the ‘Training Support Stakeholders’ group prioritized pad work, and 22% thought sparring training sessions were the best way to keep boxers engaged (Figure 2). These results imply that sustaining motivation and skill improvement requires direct, coach-like engagement, whether via guided drills, pad work, or sparring. However, with current boxing gym setups, such individualised attention is not possible as the coach to boxer ratio is uneven.

As the basis for the Value Proposition Canvas, we provide insight summaries for Boxers at Boxing Gyms and Training Support Stakeholders, emphasizing their primary tasks, challenges, and rewards.

These realizations served as the basis for the Value Proposition Canvas's Customer Profile. Each group stands for a distinct set of jobs, pains, and gains made during the interview. Focused value propositions and product features are then created that support their objectives by comprehending their unique motivations and frustrations, as revealed in later parts.

2.3 Secondary Research: Competitor Analysis

Following the identification of user insights, a competitor study was carried out to look at current boxing training programs and spot any possible market gaps. The goal of this analysis was to determine how existing goods either meet or fall short of meeting the pains, gains and jobs identified by our user interview. We determined what people value most and where discontent frequently arises by analysing the features, functioning, and user feedback of each competition. By evaluating each competitor’s features and online reviews, we can identify what users value most and where discontent commonly arises.

Three close competitors were chosen for a thorough comparison in order to guarantee relevancy; each was picked because of their high internet presence, which indicated a sizeable percentage of user adoption (Table 3). Furthermore, our competitor selection intentionally focuses on ‘robotic’ training equipment as the primary problem identified in our research is the lack of adaptive training partners. These contrasts gave us a standard by which to measure our own value proposition and design approach.

Our innovation strategy was guided by the useful design orientations and functional gaps that were discovered through the examination of features and feedback across a number of commercially available solutions (Table 4). On-screen coaching, variety of strike zones, impact sensors, and a range of training modes (e.g., cardio, sparring, mitts) are already features of existing systems, such as interactive boxing trainers and AI-powered punch trackers. These features suited to various training preferences, offered quantifiable performance statistics, and successfully increased user motivation. Nevertheless, significant drawbacks remained, such as unreliable technology, no reactive or tactile feedback, irrational attack angles, and an inability to simulate defensive situations. Furthermore, a lot of sophisticated technologies were either too expensive, lacked adaptive intelligence, or were too immobile to accommodate dynamic boxing motions like footwork.

Analysing competitors revealed that although existing solutions effectively use digital coaching and quantifiable data, they frequently fail to provide realistic engagement, adaptability, and safety for a wide range of users. Many technologies are still too expensive for general use or too inflexible for practical training. Our team used these insights to create five integrated products that each addressed a distinct market gap: Intelligent Training System, Skill Progression Studio, Adaptive Fight Intelligence, Performance Analytics, and Modular Boxing Platform. When taken as a whole, these solutions transform boxing training by fusing accessibility, realism, and data -driven feedback to benefit gym-based stakeholders as well as individual athletes.

2.4 Value Proposition Canvas

Two Value Proposition Canvases were created to reflect the unique but related demands of Boxing-Gym Boxers (Figure 3) and Training Support Stakeholders (Figure 4) after-market gaps and user insights were identified. These canvases convert the recurrent themes, frustrations, and intended results from our secondary research and interviews into focused product opportunities.

For the Boxing Gym Boxers, the findings showed that boxers had a high demand for flexibility in training, quantifiable development, and realism. Many complained about the uneven quality of the sparring-drill sessions, the lack of feedback in class, and the challenge of striking a balance between safety and growth. This influenced the development of solutions that give controlled and flexible skill development, duplicate the intensity of sparring-drill sessions without the risks involved, and provide measurable feedback to maintain motivation and progress.

Safety, structure, and scalability were the main concerns for Training Support Stakeholders. Interviews brought to light issues such a lack of gym space, expensive operating expenses, and the difficulties of sustaining participation across a range of ability levels. In order to reassure parents and boxers about the efficacy of training, stakeholders looked for methods that enhance safety oversight, standardize the quality of instruction, and offer quantifiable performance data.

The Intelligent Training System, Skill Progression Studio, Adaptive Fight Intelligence, Performance Analytics, and Modular Boxing Platform are five interconnected solutions that were designed with the use of these insights. Each addressing the challenges and successes noted in both canvases, striking a balance between the stakeholders' demands for safety, structure, and operational effectiveness and the boxers' desire for realism and advancement.

2.4.1 Problem of Interest

A basic problem that both boxers and training support stakeholders face is the absence of a thorough, secure, and flexible training program that combines the structure and quantifiability of contemporary coaching with the reality of sparring-drill sessions, according to the two Value Proposition Canvases. While boxers want flexible training, realistic involvement, and regular feedback, coaches and gym owners struggle to provide safety, personalized instruction, and efficiency in a constrained amount of time and space. Only specific elements of the training experience are addressed by current training technology. None offer a single platform that supports teaching, data tracking, and realistic skill development all at once, allowing for safe, intelligent training. This restriction causes a disconnect between what coaches can provide during training sessions and what boxers want for realistic practice.

Based on insights from interviews, surveys, and analysis of existing solutions, the proposed robot directly addresses challenges faced by boxers, including lack of training variety, misalignment with body types, and limited ways to track progress. At the same time, it enhances motivation and inclusivity by introducing interactivity and personalized feedback. Thus, our value proposition statement is defined as:

“The boxing robot delivers a safe and dynamic training experience that adapts to each individual, offering the realism of training with a training partner, and the guidance of a coach while supporting users of all levels to train effectively, build confidence, and continuously improve.”

3. Design Methodology (Zakir)

To translate the problem of interest and value propositions into actionable design outcomes, this project adopted Pahl and Beitz (Pahl, Beitz, Feldhusen, & Grote, 2007) approach to engineering design. Their framework emphasizes a logical, iterative progression from problem definition to detailed realization, ensuring that all design decisions remain traceable to user needs, competitor insights, and technical requirements.

According to Pahl and Beitz (Pahl, Beitz, Feldhusen, & Grote, 2007), there are four main stages of design:

  • Clarification of Task: Involves understanding and defining the design problem, identifying user and technical requirements, and outlining key system functions.
  • Conceptual Design: Focuses on developing alternative principles and mechanisms that could satisfy these functions.
  • Embodiment Design: Refines selected concepts, assessing feasibility, structure, and integration across subsystems.
  • Detail Design: Finalizes the chosen concept for prototyping and testing.

Each phase is supported by specific design tools developed for this project:

  • Clarification of Task → Product Needs Translation
  • Conceptual Design → Solution Principles and Concept Generation
  • Embodiment Design → Concept Screening
  • Detail Design → Prototyping and Testing

Every choice, from determining market gaps to improving technical performance, was supported by methodical reasoning through this organized design technique. It offered the engineering expertise required to turn research findings into a logical, useful, and user-focused boxing training program.

4. Final Design (Jeanette and Zakir)

4.1 System Overview (Jeanette)

Building on the identified product needs, the BoxBunny robot is organised into four key subsystems, each addressing a distinct set of functions (Figure 5):

  • User Interface – touchscreen, visual feedback, and control logic for configuring and running training sessions.
  • Upper Mechanism – includes Punch Execution (arms), Contact Points (head/torso/arms), and Impact Sensors responsible for safe, realistic striking interaction.
  • Lower Mechanism – comprises Footwork Movement (legs), Compact Footprint (base), and Height Adjustability, which together position the robot correctly relative to the user.
  • Robot Intelligence – primarily User Tracking, enabling the robot to respond to the boxer’s movement and adapt drills in real time.

Each of these subsystems is detailed in the following sections.

4.2 User Journey (Zakir)

4.2.1 End-to-End User Flow

The flowchart in Figure 6 illustrates the complete user journey when interacting with the robot, from the start of a session to its conclusion. Each activity is color-coded according to its corresponding product feature category, as indicated in the legend on the right. The session begins with height adjustment and selecting the training parameters that match the user’s goals. Once training starts, the robot functions as an intelligent training partner. The robot enables users to strike designated zones, defend against simulated punches through arm actuation, and move or orient itself in response to the user. At the end of the session, the robot generates performance analytics displayed on the dashboard. From there, users can choose to begin a new session or exit, depending on their needs.

4.2.2 Training Modes Overview

The robot offers two primary training modes: Training and Performance.

Firstly, Training Mode includes Techniques and Spar.

  • Spar features five preset boxer types that simulate common opponent styles.
  • Techniques provides three categories: Punch Combination Library, Offensive Drills, and Defensive Drills.
    • The Punch Combination Library includes multiple difficulty levels and also allows users to customise their own punch sequences.
    • o Offensive and Defensive Drills focus on developing the user’s specific attacking and defensive skills respectively.

Performance Mode measures key physical attributes such as stamina, punch power, and reaction time.

Additionally, the Others menu on the homepage enables users to adjust system settings such as switching between orthodox and southpaw, while the Insights feature provides a summary of training metrics and performance trends.

4.2.3 Movement Behavior

When the user selects the training parameters, they are interacting with the Skill Progression component of the product. This feature determines the appropriate motor actuations and software activations (such as computer vision) required for the session. As shown in Figure 7, users can choose from several training categories such as Training, Performance, and Others with each serving different purposes. For clarity, this report focuses only on the green pathway, as detailing every iteration would be unnecessarily exhaustive.

The green pathway leads to the Counter Punch Mode, which is intentionally chosen because it engages the full range of the robot’s capabilities. This mode is for all skill levels, as it replicates one of the most common foundational sparring opponents: a reactive boxer who waits for the user to initiate an attack and responds with precise, well-timed counters (typically jabs and crosses). This style is characterized by low aggression, high timing sensitivity, and reactive movement.

Translated into robot behaviour, the system uses computer vision to detect the user’s punches. For every punch thrown by the user, the robot responds with a counter-punch by actuating its arm motors. Additionally, the robot adjusts its position by moving linearly when the user steps forward or backward, and it maintains proper orientation by rotating to face the user. This ensures a realistic and continuous sparring interaction.

5.1.1. Design Specifications

The primary requirement provided was straightforward yet challenging: "as long as it is easy to use." This broad directive required translation into concrete, measurable specifications through systematic analysis of the target user base identified in Chapter 2's primary research.

User Requirements Analysis

Building on the insights gathered from our gym interviews (Section 2.2), the target user population was identified as ranging from beginners to intermediate boxers, aged 15–50, with varied levels of technical proficiency. The system therefore needed to accommodate users with minimal technical knowledge while still providing sufficient depth for more experienced practitioners. Analysis of this user base yielded several concrete usability requirements: any feature must be reachable from the main menu within a maximum of three interactions; touch targets must be at least 80×60 pixels to allow reliable operation with boxing gloves; critical information such as the current combination, score, and timer must be displayed with clear visual hierarchy; sensor input must be reflected in the interface within 500 milliseconds; and the display must maintain high contrast to remain readable during physical activity.

5.1.2. Concept Generation & Evaluation

Technology Stack Evaluation

The first critical design decision involved selecting an appropriate development framework. Given the compressed timeline, several options were evaluated and compared against key criteria including development speed, hardware compatibility, community support, and professional UI capability. The comparison is summarised in the framework evaluation table below.

PySide6 was ultimately selected as it offered the best balance of development speed—by leveraging existing Python knowledge—professional UI capabilities, and proven compatibility with the Jetson Nano platform. The extensive Qt documentation and large community support also meaningfully reduced technical risk within the compressed timeline.

Navigation Architecture Concepts

Three navigation paradigms were evaluated for the application structure, each reflecting a different design philosophy and set of trade-offs.

Concept A: Tab-Based Navigation proposed making all major sections—Training, Performance, and Settings—accessible via a persistent tab bar. While this approach offers visibility and familiarity from web and mobile conventions, it was found to be poorly suited to the 7-inch display, where limited screen real estate and the precision demands of tab targets made interaction unreliable when wearing boxing gloves. It also did not naturally accommodate multi-step workflows such as selecting a mode, configuring parameters, training, and then viewing results. This concept was therefore rejected on ergonomic grounds.

Concept B: Hierarchical Pages with Navigation Stack proposed a tree-structured page layout where users retrace their steps via a back button. This approach maximises usable screen space on each page, supports large glove-friendly buttons, and naturally models the sequential workflows inherent to boxing training. The primary concern—that deep navigation paths could leave users disoriented—was addressed by implementing an automatic navigation stack that records page history without requiring manual back button logic on every screen. This concept was selected for implementation.

Concept C: Dashboard with Modal Dialogs proposed a single dashboard screen from which all functions were launched as popup dialogs. While this eliminates page transitions, the approach was found to scale poorly given the number of training modes and their complex multi-step workflows. Modal dialogs obscure contextual information and produce a fragmented user experience. This concept was rejected as unsuitable for the feature complexity of BoxBunny.

Data Architecture Evaluation

To support multiple users sharing a single BoxBunny unit, several data isolation strategies were evaluated. The options considered and their trade-offs are detailed in the table below.

A per-user database file approach was selected as it provides complete data isolation, simplifies user management (accounts can be added or removed by creating or deleting a single folder), and preserves user privacy. Each user's data is stored in two files: users/[username]/combos.db, which records combination training progress across all 50 combos, and users/[username]/performance_history.db, which stores results from all Power, Stamina, and Reaction Time tests.

Feature Prioritisation

Given the constrained development timeline, MoSCoW prioritisation was applied to ensure that core functionality would be delivered reliably before desirable enhancements were attempted. The critical path items—user authentication, the navigation framework, combination training with CV placeholders, the database schema, and Arduino sensor integration—were designated as Must Have features and addressed in the first four iterations. Important but non-critical features such as the smart combo curriculum, performance history tracking, real-time feedback, and settings management were classified as Should Have. Desirable enhancements including AI coaching feedback, progress charts, customisable programs, and data export were placed in the Could Have category. Features requiring infrastructure beyond the project scope—cloud synchronisation, a mobile companion app, social leaderboards, and video recording—were explicitly deferred as future work.

5.1.3. Detailed Design

System Architecture

The GUI system follows a layered architecture pattern, separating concerns across five distinct layers to maximise maintainability and modularity. The Presentation Layer contains all PySide6 widgets, page classes, button handlers, and event callbacks directly visible to the user. Beneath it, the Application Layer manages navigation history via the page stack, maintains user session state, and coordinates events between pages. The Business Logic Layer houses the Combo Curriculum Engine, performance testing logic, and user management routines. The Integration Layer abstracts communication with hardware subsystems, including Arduino serial communication, the CV system interface, and ROS topic publishing for robot control. Finally, the Data Layer manages all SQLite database operations and configuration file access.

Navigation System Design

The application uses a QStackedWidget for page management, with all navigation routed through a single centralised method. A key design innovation was implementing an automatic navigation stack to track page history without requiring manual back button logic on each individual page. The navigate_to() method records the current page index before switching to the destination, and navigate_back() pops from this stack to return to whichever page the user came from. If the stack is empty, the system falls back safely to the main menu. This design eliminates code duplication, ensures consistent navigation behaviour across all 87 page transitions in the application, and allows back button visibility to be managed automatically. 

class MainWindow(QMainWindow):
    def __init__(self):
        self.pages = {}              # Dictionary: PageIndex → QWidget
        self.stacked_widget = QStackedWidget()
        self.navigation_stack = []   # Automatic page history

    def navigate_to(self, page_index, add_to_stack=True):
        """Central navigation with automatic history tracking"""
        current = self.stacked_widget.currentIndex()
        if add_to_stack and current != page_index:
            self.navigation_stack.append(current)
        self.stacked_widget.setCurrentIndex(page_index)
        self.update_back_button_visibility()

    def navigate_back(self):
        """Return to previous page using stack"""
        if self.navigation_stack:
            previous = self.navigation_stack.pop()
            self.navigate_to(previous, add_to_stack=False)
        else:
            self.navigate_to(PageIndex.MAIN_MENU, add_to_stack=False)
Combo Curriculum Engine

Rather than presenting combinations randomly, the curriculum engine applies mastery-based pedagogical principles to structure the user's progression through a library of 50 combinations. The library is organised into three difficulty tiers: 15 Beginner combinations covering single punches and 2-punch sequences; 20 Intermediate combinations introducing 3-punch sequences, body shots (using an "Xb" notation suffix), and basic defensive moves; and 15 Advanced combinations requiring 4–6 punch chains with integrated defence and counter-punching patterns. Within each tier, combinations are grouped by technique family, and the engine rotates through the current group until all combinations in that group meet the mastery threshold before unlocking the next. Mastery is defined as an average performance score of 3.0 or above over the five most recent sessions for Beginner combinations, and 4.0 or above for Intermediate and Advanced combinations. The notation system used to encode combinations is detailed in the table below.

The group-based progression algorithm works by finding the first group containing unmastered combinations and cycling through its members. For example, within the Beginner tier, Group 1 contains the six single punches (Jab through to Lead Uppercut). The system rotates through these six combinations repeatedly until each has achieved an average score of at least 3.0 over five sessions, at which point Group 2—covering 2-punch sequences beginning with the Jab—is unlocked.

Arduino Multi-Mode Protocol

To support both Power and Stamina testing using a single MPU6050 accelerometer on the Arduino, a command-based communication protocol was designed to allow the Arduino to operate in three distinct modes. In CONTINUOUS mode, acceleration data is streamed at 100 Hz for debugging and sensor tuning purposes. In STAMINA mode, the Arduino counts punches internally and returns the total count when issued a STOP command. In POWER mode, it tracks peak and average acceleration across all detected punches, returning the full metrics summary on STOP. The mode and associated command set are detailed in the table below.

The complete command and response vocabulary is as follows. Python sends mode selection commands (MODE:CONTINUOUS, MODE:STAMINA, MODE:POWER), test lifecycle commands (START, STOP), and receives structured responses including mode confirmations (OK:MODE_STAMINA), real-time events (PUNCH_DETECTED:5, POWER_PUNCH:4.2), final results (RESULT:PUNCHES:150, RESULT:PEAK:4.5,AVG:3.2,COUNT:10), and error messages (ERROR:UNKNOWN_COMMAND).

To maintain compatibility with units running earlier firmware that did not support mode commands, a protocol detection function was implemented. On connection, the GUI sends a MODE:CONTINUOUS command and listens for a response beginning with OK:. If received within one second, the command-based protocol is used; otherwise, the system falls back to a legacy streaming mode, ensuring backward compatibility without requiring firmware upgrades in the field. 

def check_protocol_version(serial_port):
    serial_port.write(b"MODE:CONTINUOUS\n")
    response = serial_port.readline(timeout=1.0)
    if response.startswith("OK:"):
        return "command_based"  # New firmware
    else:
        return "streaming"      # Old firmware, use fallback
Database Schema Design

Each user's training data is stored across two SQLite databases. The combo progress database (users/[username]/combos.db) contains a combos table recording each combination's identifier, name, sequence notation, difficulty level, current mastery score, total attempt count, and timestamps; and a performance_history table recording the score achieved in each individual training session, linked by combination ID. The performance testing database (users/[username]/performance_history.db) contains separate tables for each test type: power_tests records peak and average g-force alongside total punch count; stamina_tests records total punches, average punch rate, first- and last-30-second rates, an overall stamina score, fatigue percentage, and test duration; and reaction_tests records reaction time in seconds, accuracy as a fraction, and the counts of total and successful attempts.

5.1.4. Implementation & Testing

The GUI was developed iteratively over 15 weeks (December 2024 – April 2025), with each iteration building on the previous and resolving issues discovered through testing. Six major iterations were completed, each with defined objectives, a distinct implementation phase, structured testing, and a set of concrete outcomes.

Iteration 1: Foundation & Core Framework (Weeks 1–2, December)

The first iteration focused on establishing the development environment on the Jetson Nano, creating a basic application shell with page management, and verifying that PySide6 was viable on the target hardware. A MainWindow class was created around a QStackedWidget, with three placeholder pages (Login, Main Menu, Settings) connected via basic button navigation. Testing on the physical Jetson with the 7-inch touchscreen confirmed that touch targets met the 80×60 px minimum and that page transitions completed in approximately 200 ms. One issue discovered during this iteration was that Qt's default widget styling produced fonts and padding that were too small for reliable gloved interaction; this was resolved by implementing a custom ButtonStyle class applying 18px minimum fonts and 20px padding throughout the application. This iteration concluded with a validated proof of concept and a reusable navigation foundation for subsequent feature development.

Iteration 2: User Management & Database (Weeks 3–4, January)

The second iteration introduced multi-user support, implementing login and signup flows, a user management page, and session state tracking. Credentials were stored in a users.csv file with SHA-256 password hashing. Five test users were created to validate data persistence, login validation, and that deleting one user had no effect on others' data. An initial issue was discovered whereby deleting an account left that user's training data on disk; this was resolved by implementing cascade deletion, which removes the entire users/[username] directory when an account is deleted. By the end of this iteration, multi-user support was fully functional with zero observed data mixing across all five test accounts.

Iteration 3: Combo Curriculum System (Weeks 5–7, January–February)

The third iteration delivered the core training functionality: the 50-combo library, the mastery-based progression algorithm, and the training session UI. A ComboCurriculum class was implemented with the group-based selection algorithm described in Section 5.1.3, and the training session page was built with a countdown timer, combo display, and scoring interface. A placeholder CV integration returning a fixed score of 3.0/5.0 was used to allow full workflow testing before the CV system was available.

Three design issues were identified and resolved during this iteration. The initial curriculum design used a spaced repetition algorithm similar to Anki-style scheduling, but this was found to be poorly matched to the sequential, session-based nature of boxing practice; it was replaced with the group-based rotation approach described in the detailed design. The database was initially configured to store performance scores on a 0–1 scale, which conflicted with the CV system's 0–5 output range; the schema was migrated to use a 0–5 scale with appropriate CHECK constraints. Additionally, the original shared database design caused data mixing when multiple users trained on the same unit, which was resolved by adopting the per-user database file structure. Thirty simulated training sessions were run to validate the progression logic, confirming that combos rotated correctly within groups and that the mastery threshold calculation produced correct results.

Iteration 4: Performance Testing Suite (Weeks 8–10, February)

The fourth iteration implemented all three performance tests. The Power test was built around a power_runner.py module communicating with the Arduino at 115200 baud, detecting punches via a 2.5g threshold with 300 ms debouncing to prevent double-counting, and displaying peak g-force, average power, and total punch count on a result page. The Stamina test was implemented in stamina_runner.py as a two-minute endurance protocol, calculating punch rate over a rolling 10-second window and comparing the first and last 30 seconds to produce a fatigue analysis. The overall stamina score weights 60% on total punch volume and 40% on consistency. A simulation mode was included to enable testing without Arduino hardware. The Reaction Time test was implemented in reaction_runner.py, interfacing with Yogeeswaran's CV module to measure elapsed time between a displayed visual cue and a detected punch, also tracking accuracy across multiple attempts.

Testing validated punch detection at the 2.5g threshold on a breadboard Arduino, confirmed the stamina scoring formula produced a realistic 0–100 distribution across 10 simulated test runs, and exercised the reaction test logic with mock CV data in the 0.3–0.6 second range. The key open issue identified in this iteration—that both Stamina and Power tests shared the same Arduino connection but with no clear mode separation—was carried forward to Iteration 5 for resolution.

Iteration 5: Arduino Multi-Mode Protocol (Weeks 11–12, March)

The fifth iteration resolved the Arduino mode ambiguity by designing and implementing the command-based multi-mode protocol described in Section 5.1.3. On the firmware side, a state machine was implemented with three distinct operating modes, a command parser handling MODE:X, START, and STOP commands, structured response formatting, and 300 ms debouncing to prevent double-counting. On the Python side, check_protocol_version() was added to detect firmware capabilities, command-based measurement logic was integrated into both runner modules, and a streaming fallback was retained for backward compatibility with older firmware.

The complete mode switching sequence (CONTINUOUS → STAMINA → POWER → CONTINUOUS) was validated using the Arduino serial monitor. Backward compatibility was confirmed by testing the legacy streaming path with old firmware that did not respond to MODE commands. Punch detection accuracy reached 95%, with the remaining 5% attributed to punches falling below the 2.5g detection threshold. Two further issues were resolved: serial buffer overflow caused by Arduino output outpacing Python reads was addressed by increasing the Python read frequency to 100 Hz and clearing the buffer before each test; and occasional command misalignment was mitigated by adding retry logic with a two-second response timeout.

Iteration 6: Unified History & Navigation Stack (Weeks 13–15, March–April)

The sixth and final iteration unified performance history across all three test types, implemented the automatic navigation stack, and introduced an AI coaching feedback framework. A new performance_database.py module was created to manage a single consolidated database containing Power, Stamina, and Reaction test tables. A PerformanceHistoryPage was built displaying the latest test summary with trend indicators (↑ improved / ↓ declined / → stable), a filterable results table, and per-test trend calculations comparing each result to the previous test of the same type.

The navigation stack was fully implemented during this iteration: 87 instances of setCurrentIndex() were migrated to navigate_to(), the back button was given automatic visibility control, and deep navigation paths of six or more levels were successfully validated. The AI feedback framework was implemented as a template-based system that considers the current score, historical average, trend direction, and test-specific metrics; the integration point for a future LLM is prepared but not yet activated.

Three issues were resolved in this iteration. The back button had previously always returned to the Settings page from the History page regardless of how the user arrived; the navigation stack resolved this by returning to the actual entry point. A database schema mismatch between the 0–1 and 0–5 score scales caused CHECK constraint violations that required the database to be recreated with corrected constraints. A discrepancy between training session counts in the CSV file and combo attempts in the database was resolved by resetting the CSV history on migration to the per-user database structure.

5.1.5. Evaluation & Outcomes

Requirements Achievement

The primary requirement—"as long as it is easy to use"—was successfully met across all measurable dimensions. Maximum 3-click navigation to any feature was achieved throughout the interface; all touch targets meet the 80×60 px minimum for gloved operation; critical information is displayed with a clear visual hierarchy; the automatic navigation stack ensures consistent back button behaviour regardless of entry path; and real-time sensor feedback was measured well below the 500 ms target. Technical Achievements

Several notable technical achievements were accomplished within the four-month development window. A feature-complete GUI was delivered despite no prior PySide6 experience, demonstrating rapid technology acquisition. The layered architecture enables straightforward feature additions and maintenance, providing a robust foundation for future development. The multi-mode Arduino protocol elegantly handles three distinct sensor operating modes within a single codebase, and the navigation stack eliminates the fragile per-page back button logic that would otherwise be required across dozens of pages. Per-user database isolation ensures complete privacy and prevents data mixing, a claim validated across five test users with zero observed cross-contamination.

Design Strengths

Beyond meeting immediate requirements, several design qualities position the system well for future development. The combo curriculum is grounded in mastery learning principles rather than arbitrary selection, providing a pedagogically sound training progression. The modular architecture means that new training modes or additional performance tests can be introduced with minimal changes to existing code. All features function without internet connectivity, making the system suitable for gym environments where network access may be unreliable. Protocol detection also ensures that the GUI remains compatible with both current and legacy Arduino firmware, removing a potential barrier to hardware upgrades.

Limitations & Constraints

The development process was subject to several constraints that imposed limitations on the final system. Due to the academic timeline, it was not possible to conduct extensive usability studies with actual boxers beyond team members, meaning that real-world ergonomic feedback remains limited. All GUI work was completed by a single developer without the benefit of peer code review or collaborative refinement, which increases the risk of undiscovered edge cases. Testing was frequently performed using simulation modes rather than the fully integrated robot, as hardware access was limited during the development phase.

Technical limitations are also present. The 7-inch display constrains the amount of information that can be presented simultaneously, making side-by-side metric comparison difficult. Despite the 80×60 px touch target standard, users wearing thick gloves may still experience difficulty with some interface elements. Arduino serial communication is subject to occasional glitches that require retry logic and graceful error handling to manage. Lessons Learned

Several technical lessons emerged from this project. Framework selection proved critical: PySide6 enabled rapid development without sacrificing professional quality, validating the decision to prioritise familiarity over novelty. The modular architecture made iterative refactoring tractable; both the database migration and the navigation stack addition were completed without disrupting unrelated features. Real hardware testing proved essential for surfacing Arduino communication edge cases that simulation could not reproduce. Documenting protocol decisions early—particularly the multi-mode command structure—also saved significant debugging time when issues arose.

From a project management perspective, the MoSCoW prioritisation framework was effective in ensuring that core features were delivered reliably before enhancements were attempted. The iterative development structure allowed course corrections when requirements became clearer mid-project—most notably the shift from spaced repetition to group-based rotation—without destabilising the overall delivery. The practice of implementing CV and Arduino placeholders from the outset enabled GUI development to proceed in parallel with hardware and software work being carried out by other team members.

Future Work Recommendations

In the short term (one to two months), the most impactful enhancements would be completing the LLM integration to replace template-based AI coaching feedback with dynamically generated guidance, conducting a usability study with ten to fifteen actual boxers to gather real-world ergonomic feedback, and adding progress visualisation charts to the history page. Over a medium-term horizon (three to six months), advanced analytics for trend analysis and weak area identification, user-customisable training programmes with adjustable mastery thresholds, and data export to CSV or PDF for coaches would substantially extend the system's utility. In the longer term (six months and beyond), cloud synchronisation across multiple BoxBunny units, a mobile companion application, CV-integrated session recording with technique annotation, and community features such as global leaderboards would position BoxBunny as a more complete training ecosystem.

Conclusion

The GUI development for BoxBunny successfully delivered a comprehensive, user-friendly interface within the constrained four-month timeline of December 2024 to April 2025. The system meets all core requirements: ease of use for a broad range of users, multi-user support with complete data isolation, an intelligent training curriculum structured around mastery learning principles, a three-mode performance testing suite, and clean integration with all team members' hardware subsystems.

The iterative development approach proved essential, enabling the discovery and resolution of design issues— including database schema mismatches, navigation flow inconsistencies, and Arduino protocol ambiguity— before they could become critical failures in the final system. While limitations remain due to the compressed timeline, single-developer development, and constrained hardware access, the architecture is robust and the codebase is well-structured for future enhancement. The GUI component fulfils its role within the BoxBunny system by providing an intuitive, responsive interface that unifies the contributions of all team members into a coherent and functional training experience.
