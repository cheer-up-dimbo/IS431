3. Design Methodology (Zakir)

To translate the problem of interest and value propositions into actionable design outcomes, this project adopted Pahl and Beitz (Pahl, Beitz, Feldhusen, & Grote, 2007) approach to engineering design. Their framework emphasizes a logical, iterative progression from problem definition to detailed realization, ensuring that all design decisions remain traceable to user needs, competitor insights, and technical requirements.

According to Pahl and Beitz (Pahl, Beitz, Feldhusen, & Grote, 2007), there are four main stages of design:

  • Clarification of Task: Involves understanding and defining the design problem, identifying user and technical requirements, and outlining key system functions.
  • Conceptual Design: Focuses on developing alternative principles and mechanisms that could satisfy these functions.
  • Embodiment Design: Refines selected concepts, assessing feasibility, structure, and integration across subsystems.
  • Detail Design: Finalizes the chosen concept for prototyping and testing.

Each phase is supported by specific design tools developed for this project:

  • Clarification of Task → Product Needs Translation
  • Conceptual Design → Solution Principles and Concept Generation
  • Embodiment Design → Concept Screening
  • Detail Design → Prototyping and Testing

Every choice, from determining market gaps to improving technical performance, was supported by methodical reasoning through this organized design technique. It offered the engineering expertise required to turn research findings into a logical, useful, and user-focused boxing training program.

3.1 Clarification of Task

Insights from the competition study were first translated into specific product requirements using the Product Needs Translation tool (Table 5), which identified important enhancements, performance standards, and user experience objectives. The Function Specifications, which included explicit functions, performance standards, and design justifications for the robot's subsystems (arms, head, legs, body, skeleton, and interface), were subsequently influenced by these translated needs.

3.2 Systems Engineering V-Model

While Pahl and Beitz provides the design-stage methodology for generating and refining concepts, it does not prescribe how verification and validation activities should be structured across system levels. For this, the project adopted the Systems Engineering V-Model — a lifecycle framework that maps every decomposition step on the left side of the "V" to a corresponding integration and verification step on the right side (Forsberg, Mooz & Cotterman, 2005).

The V-Model was originally introduced by Forsberg and Mooz (1991) to address shortcomings of linear, waterfall-based system development by emphasising the critical role of verification and validation (V&V) planning concurrently with requirements decomposition, rather than as an afterthought. It has since been standardised for mechatronic product development by VDI 2206 (2004), which positions the V-Model as the macro-level process map for managing the interdisciplinary integration of mechanical, electronic, and software subsystems — precisely the challenge faced by BoxBunny.

Left Side — Decomposition and Definition

The left descending arm of the V represents the progressive decomposition of the system from high-level concept of operations down to detailed component design:

  1. Concept of Operations — establish the operational context, user needs, and system-level goals (covered in Chapters 1–2 of this report).
  2. System Requirements — derive measurable, testable requirements from the concept of operations (Section 3.1 and the performance criteria in Section 3).
  3. High-Level Design — decompose the system into subsystems and define their interfaces (Section 4 — Final Design and System Architecture).
  4. Detailed Design — design each subsystem's mechanical, electrical, and firmware components in full detail (Section 5 — Conceptual Design subsystem pages).
  5. Implementation — fabricate, assemble, and program the physical prototype.

Right Side — Integration and Recomposition

The right ascending arm mirrors the left side, with each verification level mapped to the corresponding decomposition level:

  1. Unit / Device Testing — verify individual components (motors, sensors, firmware modules) against their detailed design specifications.
  2. Subsystem Verification — verify each integrated subsystem (e.g., the rotation module, height-adjustment column, arm actuation) against the subsystem-level requirements derived from the system requirements.
  3. System Verification & Deployment — verify the fully integrated robot mechanism as a whole against the system-level requirements (RM-1 through RM-7).
  4. System Validation — validate the complete system against the original concept of operations through user testing and performance assessment.

Horizontal Traceability

The horizontal arrows connecting the left and right sides represent verification plans that are defined during the decomposition phase and executed during integration. For BoxBunny, this means that every requirement established on the left side (e.g., "remain upright under punching loads with FoS ≥ 1.5") has a pre-defined test method on the right side (e.g., "tipping test with applied load at maximum height extension").

This traceability is documented explicitly in the Robot Mechanism section, where each subsystem page links system requirements to subsystem design criteria and verification methods.
