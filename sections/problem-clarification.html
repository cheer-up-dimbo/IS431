2. Problem Clarification

2.1 Domain Overview and Trends

Boxing is one of the most widely practised combat sports globally, with participation growing across Southeast Asia, Europe, and North America. The global boxing equipment market was valued at approximately USD 4.1 billion in 2023 and is projected to grow through the decade, driven in part by the rise of boxing-based fitness among non-competitive practitioners (Mordor Intelligence, 2023).

Within sports technology, data-driven training tools have moved from elite institutions into mainstream gyms. Products such as FightCamp (FightCamp, 2017) and Hykso (Hykso, 2018) demonstrate consumer appetite for punch tracking and performance feedback. This shift has raised user expectations, as practitioners now expect quantifiable feedback and interactive training experiences that passive equipment such as bags and pads cannot provide.

Several robotic boxing products have entered the market, including the Nexersys, the STRYK RXT-1 (STRYK, 2020), and the BotBoxer by SkyTechSport (BotBoxer, 2010). While these systems offer interactive strike targets, they remain reactive, responding only to the boxer's inputs rather than initiating attacks or adapting strategy based on performance. This gap between available robotic technology and boxing-specific application is the space that BoxBunny is designed to address.

2.2 Background

Sparring is central to boxing training, allowing athletes to practise timing, defence, and adaptability under realistic conditions. However, "repetitive sub-concussive trauma from frequent and intense sparring training appears to be a significant risk for developing neurological sequelae" (Stiller, et al., 2014), creating demand for safer alternatives that replicate sparring benefits without compromising athlete well-being.

To understand this problem, we joined boxing gyms, conducted user interviews, and surveyed existing competitors. These findings informed two value proposition canvases capturing the needs and pain points of both boxers and training support stakeholders.

2.3 Primary Research: User Interviews/Surveys

2.3.1 Methodology

We conducted user interviews at six different boxing gyms in Singapore to broaden the scope of perspectives.

Our research method was guided by the Interaction Design Foundation (IDF) framework, where user interviews are carried out during the empathize phase of the design thinking process (IxDF, 2016). While at the various boxing gyms, we employed IDF's method of Unstructured Interviews, enabling a "qualitative exploration of users' thoughts and experiences" (IxDF, 2016). In addition, we also designed and sent out survey forms to get more viewpoints from boxers that we did not meet in-person from the aforementioned gyms. Through our user interview and surveys, we defined the different stakeholders in the boxing community into two groups;

- Boxing-Gym Boxers: People who train recreationally or competitively in boxing gyms are known as boxers.

- Training Support Stakeholders: Stakeholders in the training support ecosystem include parents, trainers, and gym owners who plan, coordinate, and encourage boxing involvement and progress.

Questions were tailored to each segment and refined into a concise set of guiding questions.See Appendix: User Interview Questions for the full question set used during interviews.

2.3.2 Insights from User Interviews

User interviews were conducted across multiple boxing gyms in Singapore, yielding 50 respondents representing active practitioners and 12 representing training support roles (coaches, gym owners, parents). Among boxer respondents, coaching gaps emerged as the most pressing issue: 80% cited limited coaching attention in group classes (Figure 1). This ties directly to the second-most common challenge, where 76% mentioned difficulty finding reliable training partners at matching ability levels. A related concern surfaced from 36% of respondents who reported injury risk as a deterrent to frequent sparring. Finally, 72% of boxers indicated frustration with slow progress, particularly when constrained by mixed-ability group classes that force experienced practitioners to repeat fundamentals.

2.5 Value Proposition Canvas

Two Value Proposition Canvases were created for Boxing-Gym Boxers (Figure 3) and Training Support Stakeholders (Figure 4), translating user insights and competitor gaps into targeted product opportunities.

Boxers demand flexibility, quantifiable progress, and realism. Primary frustrations centre on coaching scarcity and inadequate partner matching: 80% reported limited coaching attention, 76% struggle with training partner availability, and 72% feel constrained by slow progress in mixed-ability settings (Figure 3).

Training support stakeholders prioritise safety, structured skill development, and operational scalability. Their primary challenge is delivering individualised attention: pad work and defensive drills ranked highest in teaching importance, but current coach-to-boxer ratios prevent the required one-on-one engagement (Figure 4).

These insights informed the five design considerations that balance boxer demands for realism and progress with stakeholder requirements for safety and operational efficiency.

2.5.1 Problem of Interest

User interviews, competitor analysis, and the five design considerations (Skill Progression, Adaptive Fight Intelligence, Realistic Training Partner, Modular Boxing Platform, Performance Analytics) reveal that no existing product consolidates all five into a single, validated platform. Competitor offerings each address a subset of user needs, and none demonstrate design decisions backed by primary user research. This gap defines the problem BoxBunny aims to solve:

"How might we design an intelligent boxing training system that can dynamically and safely strike with users while assisting coaches in guiding, monitoring, and sustaining boxer development both during and beyond training sessions."

2.5.2 Value Proposition Statement

Based on insights from interviews, surveys, and analysis of existing solutions, the proposed robot directly addresses challenges faced by boxers, including lack of training variety, misalignment with body types, and limited ways to track progress. At the same time, it enhances motivation and inclusivity by introducing interactivity and personalized feedback. Thus, our value proposition statement is defined as:

"The boxing robot delivers a safe and dynamic training experience that adapts to each individual, offering the realism of training with a training partner, and the guidance of a coach while supporting users of all levels to train effectively, build confidence, and continuously improve."
