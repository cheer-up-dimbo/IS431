5.2 Upper Mechanism

The Upper Mechanism is the primary strike-delivery subsystem of the BoxBunny robotic boxing trainer. Its function is to replicate the upper-body actions of a human training partner — executing targeted punches at anatomically correct striking zones and providing real-time impact detection for training analytics. This section provides an overview of the subsystem's design rationale, scope of work, and principal contributions. Detailed documentation for each engineering domain is accessible through the sub-sections listed at the end of this page.

Design Rationale

Padwork as the Design Basis

The mechanism's specifications were derived from an analysis of traditional boxing training methods. In padwork, a coach holds mitts for the boxer to strike. The quality of the training is highly dependent on the coach's skill: an inexperienced holder may fail to provide sufficient resistance or catch the punch improperly, risking over-extension injuries such as hyperextension of the elbow. This dependency on human expertise establishes the core value proposition for robotic automation of the pad-holding function.

Complementary research into flexible training sticks identified that a lightweight, low-threat contact surface encourages more realistic defensive engagement from the boxer. This finding directly informed the selection of polyethylene foam for the training sticks, reducing the system's moment of inertia while maintaining the accuracy-training benefits of conventional mitts.

Required Striking Motions

Three distinct strike types were identified as essential for a credible training experience. The mechanism must replicate each of these motions at anatomically correct target zones.

Selected Concept: 2-DOF Coaxial Differential Joint

Three joint concepts were evaluated against criteria of training fidelity, mechanical complexity, cost, and zone obstruction. The selected solution is a 2-Degrees-of-Freedom (2-DOF) mechanism employing a coaxial differential gear arrangement with two Damiao DM-J4310-2EC brushless motors per arm. The pitch axis delivers vertical rotation (jab), while the roll axis provides horizontal rotation (hook, uppercut). Coordinated combination of both axes enables all required strike patterns from a single compact joint. The full concept selection analysis — including a decision matrix comparing three candidate designs — is presented in Design & Ideation.

Mass Centralisation

A critical advantage of the coaxial configuration is motor mass centralisation. Both Damiao motors are mounted side-by-side at the base of the arm assembly, each driving a pinion gear that meshes with the central coaxial gear stack, rather than being distributed along the arm's length. This concentrates the heaviest components at the pivot point, thereby drastically reducing the arm's moment of inertia. A lower moment of inertia demands proportionally less torque to achieve a given angular acceleration, which is the primary reason the system can attain human-competitive punch velocities (target: 90° in ≤ 0.25 s) without exceeding the mechanical stress limits of its 3D-printed drivetrain components.

The Differential Coupling Challenge

The coaxial arrangement introduces a non-trivial kinematic coupling between axes. Because the pitch assembly is housed inside the roll-driven outer housing, any rotation of the roll axis (Motor 2) carries the pitch mechanism with it. The internal bevel gear effectively "walks" around the drive gear, producing an unwanted parasitic pitch change proportional to the roll displacement. If left uncompensated, a pure roll command would cause the training stick to tilt vertically — invalidating the intended strike trajectory.

The firmware must therefore mathematically cancel this parasitic coupling in real-time. When a pure roll motion is commanded, both Motor 1 (Pitch) and Motor 2 (Roll) are driven at identical speeds and displacements, preventing the bevel gear from walking. The closed-form inverse kinematic equations that govern this compensation are derived in the Mechanical Design sub-section.

Engineering Highlights

The following subsections present the three most significant engineering outcomes of the Upper Mechanism development. Each represents a transition from initial naive implementation to a validated, robust solution — demonstrating the iterative problem-solving methodology central to this project.

From Stateless Motion to a Dynamic Combat Orbit

Early motion testing employed a stateless command model: each strike was executed as an independent point-to-point movement, with the arm returning to a fixed neutral ("zero") position between every action. This produced mechanically correct but visually robotic behaviour: Punch → Return to Zero → Pause → Punch. The resulting motion lacked the fluidity and pressure of a real sparring partner.

This limitation motivated the development of the Combat Orbit FSM (Dynamic Sparring engine). The Combat Orbit treats the arm's position as a continuous state within a dynamic guard envelope rather than resetting to a fixed origin. Three algorithms work together to produce fluid, uninterrupted sparring:

  1. Vector Alignment Skip Logic: Before executing a windup, the FSM computes the angular alignment between the arm's current approach vector and the intended strike vector. If the alignment angle falls below a configurable threshold (default: 30°), the windup phase is skipped entirely — the arm proceeds directly to the apex. This eliminates unnecessary retractions when consecutive strikes share similar trajectories.
  2. M1-Based Perimeter Transit Routing: When a windup is required and the direct path would cross the robot's centre-line (the user's body zone), the router inserts intermediate via-points from the strike library, forcing the arm to orbit along the perimeter of the workspace.
  3. Proportional Snap-Back Recovery: After each apex, the arm retracts to a dynamically computed recovery position — a configurable fraction (λ ∈ [0.1, 1.0]) of the apex-to-windup vector — maintaining the guard without returning to zero.

The combined effect transforms the system from a coordinate-following actuator into a reactive sparring partner that maintains spatial pressure on the user. Full implementation details and the FSM decision pipeline are documented in Firmware & Software.

Multi-Layered Safety Architecture

Protecting 3D-printed drivetrain components and the user during high-velocity sparring required safety measures distributed across multiple system layers rather than relying on any single mechanism. The following three-tier architecture was implemented:

Layer Mechanism Response Time Purpose
1. Hardware Damiao built-in current limiter < 1 ms Absolute overcurrent protection at the motor controller level
2. Firmware Software dI/dt monitoring (Teensy 4.0) 5 ms (one loop cycle) Gear-corrected current threshold with configurable limits; detects mechanical obstruction before hardware limits are reached
3. Application Kinematic auto-retract (GUI V3) ~50 ms Proportional snap-back from apex after each strike; prevents sustained contact force

This layered approach ensures that no single software bug or communication failure can result in uncontrolled motor torque being applied to the mechanism or the user. The firmware layer is particularly significant: by monitoring the current derivative (dI/dt) rather than absolute current alone, the system detects mechanical obstructions — such as the arm striking an object or reaching a mechanical endstop — within a single 5 ms loop cycle, before the forces are large enough to damage the 3D-printed gears.

Case Study: Regenerative Braking & PSU Overvoltage

During integration testing, abrupt motor deceleration at the end of a high-speed strike caused the Damiao motors to operate momentarily as generators, injecting back-EMF onto the 24V bus. When the transient voltage exceeded the PSU's overvoltage protection (OVP) threshold (~28V), the supply tripped and all subsystems — including the Teensy microcontroller and Jetson compute module — lost power simultaneously.

Two mitigation strategies were implemented:

  1. Software PWM Ramp-Down: A _ramp_down() method issues six progressively decreasing PWM commands over 300 ms before the final STOP, limiting the rate of magnetic flux collapse and preventing the back-EMF spike from reaching the OVP threshold.
  2. Dual-Rail Power Isolation: The 24V motor bus and the 12V logic rail were separated onto independent power supplies. This architectural change ensures that even if the motor bus experiences a transient OVP trip, the Teensy and Jetson remain powered — preventing mid-session firmware reboots and loss of encoder state.

A permanent hardware solution (TVS clamp or Active Shunt Regulator on the 24V bus) is documented as a recommended future improvement. The full defect analysis is presented in Troubleshooting & Iteration.

Summary of Key Contributions

The following list summarises the principal engineering contributions of the Upper Mechanism development:

  1. Coaxial Differential Kinematics with Mass Centralisation: Closed-form forward and inverse kinematic equations were derived for the coupled 2-DOF joint, incorporating real-time parasitic pitch compensation. Motor mass centralisation at the pivot reduces moment of inertia, enabling human-competitive strike velocities.
  2. Combat Orbit FSM: A continuous-state sparring engine replaced stateless point-to-point motion, integrating vector alignment skip logic, perimeter transit routing, and proportional snap-back to produce fluid, uninterrupted combat sequences.
  3. Multi-Layered Safety Architecture: Three independent protection tiers — hardware current limiting, firmware dI/dt obstruction detection, and application-level kinematic auto-retract — provide defence-in-depth for both the drivetrain and the user.
  4. IMU-Based Strike Analytics: A Quad-IMU data acquisition pipeline — initially validated on an isolated rig at 500 Hz — was merged into the unified firmware for real-time punch detection via L2 acceleration vector norm thresholding.
  5. Regenerative Braking Mitigation & Dual-Rail Isolation: Software ramp-down and power rail separation resolved PSU OVP trips during high-speed sparring, ensuring uninterrupted system operation.
  6. Systematic Defect Resolution: Nine cross-layer integration defects — spanning CAN protocol parsing, I2C bus reliability, micro-ROS transport recovery, and regenerative braking — were diagnosed and resolved through structured root-cause analysis.
