Drive Architecture

When the design shifted from the geared 011.25.400 slewing ring to the non-geared 010.10.120, an external drive mechanism became necessary. This page documents the selection of a timing-belt transmission over a pinion-gear drive, the pulley/belt sizing rationale, and the torque path from motor to rotating platform.

Why Timing Belt over Pinion

With the original geared slewing ring abandoned, two external drive options were evaluated:

Criterion Direct Pinion on Outer Ring Timing Belt
Motor placement Must be adjacent to bearing — constrained packaging Motor on fixed base — flexible positioning
Ratio tuning Fixed by gear-tooth counts; requires new gears to change Adjustable via pulley selection
Noise & smoothness Metal-on-metal gear mesh noise Quiet operation; belt absorbs minor vibrations
Backlash Gear tooth clearance introduces backlash Positive engagement with proper tensioning
Packaging Pinion must mesh at bearing OD; limits available space Belt routes around obstacles; tensioner adjustable
Low-speed torque Good Good — timing belts handle low-speed, high-torque well

The timing belt was selected because it allows the motor to remain on the fixed base (not on the rotating structure), offers flexible ratio tuning through pulley selection, simplifies packaging, and supports smooth low-speed torque transmission.

Inner Ring as Controlled Surface

A key architectural decision is which ring of the slewing bearing rotates. In the final design, the inner ring is fixed to the base structure and the outer ring rotates, carrying the platform above. The timing belt wraps around the outer ring's circumference, with the driven pulley effectively being the outer ring itself (or a pulley bonded/bolted to it).

This arrangement ensures that:

  • The motor and belt tensioner are mounted to the non-rotating base, simplifying wiring and maintenance
  • The entire rotating assembly (platform, height column, upper body) turns as a single unit
  • Belt tension adjustments can be made from the accessible fixed side

Pulley & Belt Sizing

The belt drive system consists of three key elements:

  • Small driving pulley on the motor shaft
  • Large driven pulley (or belt-engagement surface) on the outer ring
  • Idler/tensioner pulley to maintain belt tension and wrap angle

The gear ratio is determined by the ratio of driven to driving pulley diameters. Because the outer ring of the 010.10.120 bearing serves as the driven surface, the effective driven diameter is approximately equal to the bearing's outer diameter, providing a large reduction ratio from a small motor pulley.

Baseline Calculations

With a target yaw speed of ~150°/s at the platform:

ωout = 150°/s ≈ 2.62 rad/s ≈ 25 rpm

The required motor speed depends on the selected gear ratio (pulley diameter ratio). For example, with a ~5:1 reduction, the motor must run at approximately 124 rpm — a reasonable speed range for BLDC servo motors.

The required motor torque is:

Tmotor = Tplatform / (GR × η)

where Tplatform is the required torque at the rotating platform, GR is the gear ratio, and η is the belt transmission efficiency (~0.95 for a well-tensioned timing belt).

Torque Path

The complete torque path from motor input to platform rotation:

  1. BLDC servo motor produces rotary torque at the input shaft. Motor selection and control are documented by the upper-assembly agent.
  2. Small timing pulley on the motor shaft converts motor torque to belt tension.
  3. Timing belt transmits force from driving to driven pulley with minimal backlash and good efficiency.
  4. Outer ring of slewing bearing receives belt force, producing yaw torque about the bearing's vertical axis.
  5. Rotating platform and everything above it (height column, torso, arms) rotates as a single rigid body.

Tensioning

The idler/tensioner pulley is positioned to maintain adequate belt tension and wrap angle on the small driving pulley. Proper tensioning is critical because:

  • Under-tensioned belts allow tooth skip under shock loading (punches)
  • Over-tensioned belts increase bearing side-load and accelerate wear
  • The tensioner must be adjustable to account for belt stretch over time

Requirement Traceability

The timing-belt drive architecture directly addresses system-level requirement RM-3 (Yaw Rotation): the robot must rotate on the spot to present variable attack angles to the user. The mechanical design ensures:

  • Smooth, low-backlash transmission via positive-engagement timing belt
  • Motor mounting on the fixed base — simplifying wiring and serviceability
  • Adjustable gear ratio through pulley selection to match the required yaw speed
  • Adequate stiffness to hold commanded angles under punching disturbances

The corresponding verification method — yaw speed measurement and backlash characterisation under loaded conditions — is described in the Robot Mechanism verification plan. Electrical integration and motor control implementation are documented separately by the upper-assembly agent.
