Bearing Selection & Iteration

The rotation subsystem passed through a major bearing-level revision. This page documents the full evaluation of bearing families, the original geared slewing ring concept, and the design pivot to a smaller non-geared bearing that ultimately enabled the timing-belt drive architecture.

Bearing Requirements

The yaw rotation bearing must support the full BoxBunny robot mass (≈ 20–25 kg), plus dynamic punching loads and overturning moments from the elevated torso and arms. Specific requirements:

  • Carry combined axial, radial and moment loads from punches and body weight
  • Provide high rotational stiffness so the robot does not "wobble" or twist when struck
  • Remain durable under repeated shock and vibration
  • Allow reasonably precise positioning when driven by an external motor
  • Be cost-effective and readily available for a student prototype
  • Fit within the limited height of the lower structure

Bearing Families Evaluated

Lazy-Susan Bearing

Inexpensive, thin-section turntable bearings used in furniture and light-duty applications. While very low cost and compact, they are designed for light axial loads with limited overturning-moment capacity. Minimal preload results in noticeable play in tilt and rotation — unsuitable for precise yaw positioning under punching loads.

Verdict: Rejected — insufficient stiffness and moment capacity.

Four-Point Contact Ball Slewing Ring

Single-row ball bearings with specially profiled raceways allowing each ball to carry load at up to four contact points. These bearings are designed for combined axial, radial and overturning moment loads, including shock loads. They can be preloaded to reduce clearance, provide lower friction than cross-roller designs, and are widely available at reasonable cost.

Verdict: Selected — best balance of load capacity, stiffness, availability and cost.

Cross-Roller Slewing Ring

Cylindrical rollers in alternating X-pattern orientations provide the highest tilting and rotational stiffness of all evaluated options. Commonly used in robot joints and precision rotary stages. However, they are more expensive and less available than four-point ball types, with performance exceeding what is strictly required.

Verdict: Rejected — over-specified; cost and availability not justified.

Thrust Ball Slewing Ring

Optimised mainly for axial loads with limited radial and moment capacity. Not suited for the large overturning moments generated by an elevated torso under punching impacts — would require additional bearings, increasing complexity.

Verdict: Rejected — does not match combined-load demands.

Decision Matrix

The four-point contact ball slewing ring bearing meets BoxBunny's load and stiffness requirements with margin, while offering the best cost-performance and procurement practicality.

Bearing Iteration: 011.25.400 → 010.10.120

The original design concept used a large geared slewing ring bearing (011.25.400) with external gear teeth. This would have combined structural support and gear drive in a single component — an attractive integration concept.

011.25.400 — Rejection Rationale

Issue Impact
Excessive weight Large ring diameter and integral gear teeth add significant mass to the lower structure
High shipping cost Overseas procurement of a large, heavy specialty bearing is expensive
Integration difficulty Large bolt-circle diameter requires a correspondingly large mounting plate, expanding the base footprint
Motor torque demand The Module 5 external gear with small pinion requires high motor torque due to the large driven-gear inertia

010.10.120 — Selected Bearing

The design shifted to the smaller, more practical 010.10.120 non-geared slewing ring. This bearing retains the four-point contact ball architecture (combined load capability, preloadability, moderate cost) but eliminates the integral gear teeth. The consequences of this change:

  • Reduced weight and cost — smaller diameter, no gear machining.
  • Simpler integration — smaller bolt circle fits within the existing base structure.
  • Drive system redesign required — without integral gear teeth, an external drive mechanism must be introduced. This led directly to the timing-belt drive architecture.
