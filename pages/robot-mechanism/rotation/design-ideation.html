Design Ideation

This page documents the conceptual selection for the rotation subsystem, determining the overall motion architecture, the central bearing type, and the actuation motor.

1. Motion Axis Selection

Having to accomplish two motions (translation and rotation), a decision had to be made to either have both controlled within one mechanism (e.g., omnidirectional wheels) or decouple those axes into separate control systems.

Omnidirectional Wheels

The omnidirectional wheel concept provides full planar mobility of the robot base. A single drive system enables translations in both the X and Y directions, as well as on-the-spot rotation, by appropriately commanding the individual wheel speeds. While attractive for general mobile robotics, it presents critical limitations for a punch-receiving boxing robot on typical gym flooring (mats, rubber tiles):

  • Wheels can sink slightly into the surface, changing the effective rolling radius and increasing rolling resistance.
  • The floor itself adds a compliant layer beneath the robot, amplifying pose creep under repeated impacts.
  • Small shifts accumulate over rounds, making it difficult to maintain a consistent stance reference relative to the user.

Decoupled Axes (Selected)

A design that explicitly decouples the primary motions (a linear rail for the forward–backward axis combined with a slewing bearing for rotation) sacrifices omnidirectional mobility but introduces significant advantages: high rotational stiffness, well-defined backlash, and high moment capacity, all independent of floor conditions.

2. Bearing Selection

The yaw rotation system must support the full BoxBunny robot mass (≈ 20–25 kg), plus dynamic punching loads and overturning moments from the elevated torso and arms. Four bearing families were evaluated.

  • Lazy-Susan Bearing: Very low cost, but intended mainly for light axial loads with minimal preload. The noticeable play in tilt and rotation makes it unsuitable for precise yaw positioning or repeated impacts.
  • Cross-Roller Slewing Ring: Very high tilting and rotational stiffness due to line contact. Excellent for combined loads, but generally more expensive and less readily available than ball-types. Higher performance than strictly required.
  • Thrust Ball Slewing Rings: Optimised mainly for axial loads; not optimised for large overturning moments from an elevated torso without requiring additional complex bearings.
  • Four-Point Contact Ball Slewing Ring (Selected): Designed to support combined axial, radial, and overturning moment loads. It can be preloaded to reduce internal clearance, and is widely available in standard sizes. It offers a perfect balance of load capacity, stiffness, availability, and cost.

3. Motor Selection

The yaw motor must rotate the full upper body about the vertical axis while resisting punching-induced moments. It must achieve a target speed of ≈150°/s with accurate, repeatable positioning and good disturbance rejection.

  • Brushed DC Gearmotor: High torque at low output speed and very simple control. However, it lacks inherent position control, and gearbox backlash causes visible yaw "looseness" when the robot is punched.
  • Closed-Loop Stepper (Hybrid Servo): Good low-speed torque and encoder feedback for correcting position errors. However, steppers have torque ripple and microstep non-linearity, producing small angle "cogging" that can be felt as chatter by the user.
  • BLDC Servo Motor with Encoder (Selected): Smooth, sinusoidal torque production via field-oriented control (FOC) reduces cogging and vibration. Closed-loop position control provides high yaw accuracy and active disturbance rejection under punching.

For the yaw axis, user perception and stability are critical: the robot should rotate smoothly and feel "solid" under punches, without rattling or drifting. A BLDC Servo Motor is therefore chosen to provide controlled yaw motion and high positional stiffness.
