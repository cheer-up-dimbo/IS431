5.2.1 Base

The base subsystem forms the foundation of the entire BoxBunny robot. It was developed to satisfy two high-level requirements simultaneously: stability under punching loads (RM-1) and safe compactness for user footwork (RM-2). BoxBunny operates close to a boxer, so the base cannot simply be enlarged in all directions for brute-force stability. It must remain compact and non-obstructive near the user's lead foot while still resisting forward tipping when the upper body is struck.

Requirements & Considerations

The following system-level requirements directly govern the base subsystem design:

  • RM-1 (Stability): The base must prevent toppling under worst-credible punching loads with a factor of safety ≥ 1.5. Tipping is treated as the governing failure mode, not sliding.
  • RM-2 (Compact Footprint): The footprint must preserve footwork space and reduce injury risk. Boxing gyms are space-constrained with multiple users training in parallel.
  • RM-7 (Portability): The base must allow transport by 1–2 persons between venues. Transport features must not compromise the planted operational stance.

These requirements led directly to the need for a geometry that protects footwork space, keeps the robot upright under worst-credible punches, and allows the entire machine to be moved conveniently between use locations.

Design

Trapezoidal Platform Geometry

The final base solution is a welded trapezoidal steel-tube platform with a narrower front and wider rear, inspired partly by the geometry and portability logic of commercial gym equipment such as benches. The trapezoidal shape was chosen because it gives the boxer more clearance at the front and sides, reducing the chance of stepping on or colliding with the base during pivots and footwork. At the same time, the wider rear region provides more area for structural support, lower-mechanism mounting, optional ballast, and transport features.

Tipping as the Governing Failure Mode

A major design consideration was the decision to treat tipping as the governing failure mode, not sliding. The stability requirement was established using overturning mechanics: the punch was converted into an overturning moment about the front edge of the base, while the self-weight of the robot created a restoring moment through the centre of gravity. A moderate factor of safety of approximately 1.5 was selected to account for uncertainty in punch intensity, strike height, impact direction, modelling simplifications, and fabrication tolerances. Floor friction was recognised as helpful in practice, but intentionally excluded from the primary stability criterion so that the base would remain inherently stable through geometry and mass distribution alone.

Portability and Deployment

Because the robot must be moved between storage, fabrication, and public demonstration settings, the base was developed with guided transport wheels and handle logic, drawing inspiration from movable gym benches. The rear-mounted RMNA100, 100 mm wheels mounted using KCLSBF12-62 pins complement the wider rear footprint and allow the system to be tipped and rolled without disturbing the compact operational stance.

Steel Fabrication and Corrosion Mitigation

The base uses welded SHS/RHS steel members arranged in the trapezoidal footprint. Rust was treated as a real design issue, not a cosmetic one. Interior and exterior steel surfaces use different protection strategies because enclosed SHS interiors cannot be fully cleaned after assembly. Interior surfaces rely on penetrating, non-drying protective coatings, while exterior surfaces use mechanical rust removal, anticorrosion primer, and paint topcoats. Full galvanising was considered but rejected as impractical at prototype scale.

Validation

At the subsystem level, the base concept satisfies the most important requirements through geometry-driven design and transparent hand calculations. The selected trapezoidal geometry addresses the user-space requirement by reducing front obstruction and preserving room for pivots and stance changes. The structural choice of welded SHS/RHS members provides a rigid foundation for the rotating and height-adjustment mechanisms.

Most importantly, the stability criterion was validated using overturning-moment versus restoring-moment comparisons about the front edge pivot, rather than relying solely on simulation or optimistic floor friction assumptions.

Current validation is strongest in terms of stability logic, footprint selection, and packaging intent. Physical verification should continue through prototype tipping tests, centre-of-gravity confirmation, and practical transport assessment with the final assembled mass.
