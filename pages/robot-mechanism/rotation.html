5.2.2 Rotation

The rotation subsystem reproduces yaw re-orientation, one of the most important footwork behaviours in live boxing drills. A coach does not only move forward and backward; they also pivot and re-angle to create new attack lines, defensive responses, and counter-punch opportunities. For BoxBunny, this required a dedicated yaw axis capable of rotating the upper structure on the spot while remaining stable under repeated impact.

Requirements & Considerations

The rotation subsystem requirements were decomposed from the broader footwork problem into two essential motions: forward-backward translation and yaw rotation. The final architecture intentionally decouples these two motions. The yaw mechanism must:

  • Hold commanded angle under impact (RM-1)
  • Provide moderate speed with good torque multiplication, targeting ~150°/s angular velocity (RM-4)
  • Remain compact near the boxer's working zone (RM-2)
  • Carry combined axial, radial, and overturning loads
  • Integrate at the bottom of the stack so the heaviest rotary support remains closest to the ground

The decoupled yaw stage was justified over omnidirectional wheels because floor-dependent wheel slip and pose creep are particularly unsuitable for a punch-receiving training robot.

Design

Final Architecture: Low-Profile Yaw Module

The final rotation solution is a low-profile yaw module built around a non-geared slewing ring bearing, external timing-belt drive, perimeter support rollers, and integrated rear transport wheels.

  • At the centre: a circular rotating top plate supported by a 010.10.120 slewing bearing
  • Torque delivered by an off-axis motor through a timing belt with small pulley, larger driven pulley, and idler/tensioner
  • Around the outer edge: cam-follower roller assemblies for tilt stabilisation under off-axis punching
  • At the rear: RMNA100, 100 mm transport wheels on KCLSBF12-62 pins for tip-and-roll transport

Design Evolution

The design passed through a major bearing-level revision. An earlier concept considered a large geared slewing ring bearing (011.25.400), which would have combined structural support and gear drive in a single component. Although structurally capable, it was too heavy, too costly to ship, difficult to integrate, and demanding in motor torque. The design shifted to the smaller, more practical 010.10.120 non-geared slewing ring.

This bearing change forced a second innovation: the drive system moved to a modular external timing-belt transmission. Compared with direct pinion drive, the belt allows the motor to remain on the fixed base, offers flexible ratio tuning through pulley selection, simplifies packaging, and supports smooth low-speed torque transmission.

A third innovation was the addition of cam-follower edge supports. Rather than forcing the central bearing alone to resist all rocking effects, the system shares this task with discrete perimeter rollers, increasing the effective support radius.

Electrical & Control Architecture

The rotation axis is driven by a Z55BLD400-24GU 400 W BLDC motor with a 25:1 planetary gearbox, controlled via the ZBLD C20-800LRC CAN motor driver. Position feedback is provided by an AS5047P 14-bit magnetic encoder (SPI).

The motor controller is an Arduino Uno R4 WiFi, operating as a separate controller from the Teensy 4.0 that manages the arm motors. Communication with the Jetson host uses WiFi UDP — eliminating physical cable routing through the rotating base joint, which would otherwise fatigue and fail. The CAN bus operates at 125 kbps (vs 1 Mbps for the arm motors), necessitating a separate bus to avoid firmware complexity. Full details are documented in the Electrical & Control sub-page.

Validation

The final rotation-base assembly satisfies the main subsystem requirements in concept and integration. It preserves the decoupled-axis architecture, keeps the yaw stage at the bottom of the robot stack, and uses a slewing-bearing-supported rotating plate capable of carrying combined loads near the ground. The modular belt-drive arrangement remains consistent with the requirement for a smooth, reduced, motorised yaw axis targeting approximately 150°/s.

Current validation is strongest in concept evaluation, bearing selection trade-off, CAD integration, and load-path reasoning. Remaining verification work includes: physical plate-stiffness checks, cam-follower contact validation, belt compliance under reversal, and confirmation that transport-wheel geometry does not interfere with the working footprint.
