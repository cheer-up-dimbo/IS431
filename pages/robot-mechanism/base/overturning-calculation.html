Overturning Calculation

This page presents the stability analysis that governs the base subsystem design. Tipping is treated as the governing failure mode — not sliding — because the consequences of the robot toppling onto a user are far more severe than a controlled slide.

Tipping as the Governing Failure Mode

When a punch impacts the robot's upper body, two potential failure modes arise at the base:

  1. Sliding: The entire robot translates along the floor. This requires the horizontal component of the punch force to exceed the static friction between the base and the floor.
  2. Tipping: The robot rotates about the front edge of the base (the pivot). This occurs when the overturning moment from the punch exceeds the restoring moment from the robot's self-weight.

Tipping was selected as the governing criterion because:

  • Floor friction is highly variable — rubber gym mats, concrete, tiles, and interlocking foam all provide different friction coefficients, making sliding analysis unreliable as a primary safety criterion.
  • Tipping is geometry- and mass-dependent, which are properties the designer can control directly, independent of deployment conditions.
  • A robot that is inherently stable against tipping through geometry and mass distribution alone will also be resistant to tipping on any floor surface, without relying on favourable friction.

Moment Analysis

The stability analysis is performed by comparing overturning and restoring moments about the front edge (pivot line) of the trapezoidal base. This is the worst-case pivot because frontal punches create the largest overturning moment about this edge.

Overturning Moment

The overturning moment Mover is generated by the horizontal component of the punch force acting at a height above the base:

Mover = Fpunch × hstrike

where Fpunch is the worst-credible horizontal punch force and hstrike is the height of the strike point above the pivot line (front edge of base).

For design purposes, the worst-credible punch force was estimated from published biomechanics data for recreational and intermediate boxers. Peak forces of up to ~1.5 kN are typical for heavy straight punches from average-sized users.

Restoring Moment

The restoring moment Mrest is generated by the robot's self-weight acting through the centre of gravity, measured horizontally from the front-edge pivot:

Mrest = Wtotal × dCG

where Wtotal is the total weight of the robot (including ballast if present) and dCG is the horizontal distance from the front edge to the projection of the centre of gravity.

Go/No-Go Logic

The base design is stable if the restoring moment exceeds the overturning moment by the required factor of safety:

FoS = Mrest / Mover ≥ 1.5

If this criterion is satisfied, the base will not tip under the worst-credible punch loading. If FoS falls below 1.5, the designer must either increase dCG (by shifting mass rearward or adding ballast) or reduce hstrike (by lowering the target height).

Factor of Safety Justification

A moderate factor of safety of 1.5 was selected to account for the following uncertainties:

Uncertainty Source Effect
Punch intensity variation Users range from beginners (~200 N) to experienced boxers (~1.5 kN)
Strike height variation Head strikes produce larger lever arms than torso strikes
Impact direction Off-axis punches (hooks, angled straights) reduce frontal overturning but introduce lateral components
Modelling simplifications CG location estimated from CAD; actual mass distribution may differ after assembly
Fabrication tolerances Weld distortion, material variation, and assembly alignment affect actual geometry

Floor friction was intentionally excluded from the primary stability criterion. While friction helps resist both sliding and tipping in practice, it was not credited because its value is unpredictable across deployment environments. The base must be inherently stable through geometry and mass distribution alone.

Requirement Traceability

This overturning analysis verifies compliance with system-level requirement RM-1 (Structural Stability): the robot must remain upright under worst-credible punching loads without relying on external anchoring. The go/no-go criterion (FoS ≥ 1.5) provides a quantitative pass/fail gate that can be re-evaluated whenever subsystem masses or heights change.

The analysis is also coupled to RM-7 (Portability): adding ballast to increase the restoring moment directly increases the robot's transport mass. The trapezoidal base geometry (documented in Stability & Footprint Selection) optimises this trade-off by biasing the self-weight rearward rather than increasing total mass.

The corresponding verification method — prototype tipping tests with increasing applied force — is described in the Robot Mechanism verification plan.
