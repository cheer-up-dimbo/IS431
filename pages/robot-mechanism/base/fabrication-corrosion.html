Fabrication & Corrosion

The base is constructed from welded SHS/RHS steel members arranged in the trapezoidal footprint. This page documents the structural material choices, wall thickness rationale, and the differentiated corrosion-protection strategy for interior and exterior surfaces.

Steel Structure

The base frame uses Square Hollow Section (SHS) and Rectangular Hollow Section (RHS) mild steel tubing, joined by MIG welding. SHS/RHS was selected over solid bar, angle iron, or channel because:

  • High second moment of area per unit weight: Hollow sections resist bending and torsion more efficiently than equivalent-weight solid sections.
  • Flat external faces: Enable straightforward mounting of plates, brackets, and mechanisms without machining.
  • Availability: SHS/RHS is readily available in standard sizes from local steel suppliers.
  • Weldability: Mild steel SHS/RHS is straightforward to MIG-weld, with well-understood joint preparation procedures.

SHS Wall Thickness

Larger SHS members in the base are specified with a minimum wall thickness of 6 mm. This sizing accounts for:

  • Weld penetration: Thicker walls ensure adequate fusion depth at butt and fillet welds, avoiding cold welds on thin-wall sections.
  • Mounting loads: The SHS members support bolted connections for the slewing bearing, linear stage, and height-adjustment column — all of which introduce concentrated loads at bolt holes.
  • Impact resistance: The base must resist transmitted punching loads without permanent deformation at connection points.

Corrosion as a Design Issue

Rust was treated as a real design issue, not a cosmetic one. The robot operates in gym environments where humidity, sweat, and cleaning chemicals create a mildly corrosive atmosphere. Left unprotected, mild steel structural members would develop surface rust within weeks, leading to progressive section loss, unsightly appearance, and potential staining of gym floors.

The corrosion protection strategy differentiates between interior and exterior surfaces because they present fundamentally different treatment challenges.

Interior Surface Protection

Enclosed SHS interiors cannot be fully cleaned, sanded, or painted after assembly. Weld spatter, mill scale, and residual moisture are trapped inside the tubes. The interior protection strategy uses:

  • Penetrating, non-drying protective coatings (e.g. lanolin-based rust preventatives or cavity wax) applied by spraying or pouring into the tube ends before final welding and sealing.
  • These coatings creep along surfaces, displace moisture, and form a long-term barrier without requiring surface preparation to a high standard.

This approach is borrowed from automotive and marine practice, where box-section chassis members are routinely treated with cavity wax to prevent internal corrosion.

Exterior Surface Protection

Exterior steel surfaces are accessible for conventional surface preparation and coating. The treatment sequence is:

  1. Mechanical rust removal: Wire brushing, sanding, or grinding to remove mill scale, weld spatter, and any existing surface corrosion. Target standard: SA 2½ (near-white metal) or equivalent hand preparation.
  2. Anticorrosion primer: Red oxide or zinc-rich primer applied within hours of surface preparation to prevent flash rusting. Two coats are applied with adequate drying time between layers.
  3. Paint topcoat: Enamel or polyurethane topcoat for UV resistance, abrasion protection, and aesthetic finish.

Galvanising Rejection

Full hot-dip galvanising was considered as a comprehensive corrosion solution. However, it was rejected as impractical at prototype scale for the following reasons:

  • Dimensional distortion: The high-temperature zinc bath (≈450 °C) can warp thin-wall welded assemblies, particularly where residual welding stresses are present.
  • Drainage and venting: All enclosed sections must be drilled with vent and drain holes to prevent steam explosions during immersion — this compromises the sealed tube integrity.
  • Cost and turnaround: Commercial galvanising facilities typically charge per-dip with minimum batch sizes, making single-prototype treatment disproportionately expensive.
  • Post-galvanising difficulty: Welding or modifying galvanised steel produces toxic zinc fumes requiring specialised ventilation, and damages the zinc layer at the weld zone.

The combination of primer-and-paint (exterior) with cavity wax (interior) provides adequate protection for the prototype's expected service life without the constraints and risks of galvanising.
