5.2.3 Height Adjustment

The height-adjustment subsystem enables BoxBunny to accommodate users of different heights without compromising the robot's rigidity during training. From the user's perspective, the workflow is straightforward: select a suitable target height, the robot adjusts vertically, and it must then remain stable throughout operation without visible wobble, jamming, or misalignment.

Requirements & Considerations

The user need for height adaptability was translated into three subsystem-level requirements, cascading from the system-level requirement RM-3:

  1. Provide a 400 mm vertical stroke so head and torso target zones can be repositioned across the intended user height range.
  2. Adjustment must be practically fast for setup use — the engineering target is approximately 32 seconds over the 400 mm stroke (≈ 0.75 m/min), constrained by the selected screw-jack architecture.
  3. Preserve structural performance under boxing-related disturbances. The height-adjustment system sits directly within a major load path beneath the upper body, so vertical actuation and lateral load resistance must be treated as separate functions.

Design

Custom Telescopic Lift Column

The final height-adjustment solution is a custom telescopic lift column actuated by a motorised travelling-nut screw jack. This subsystem combines a structural guiding column with a dedicated axial lifting mechanism:

  • 8080 aluminium extrusion — moving inner member
  • Delrin wear pads — sacrificial low-friction guide surfaces
  • Welded steel outer tube with integrated plate — fixed sleeve
  • HK2T screw jack with travelling nut — axial lifting device

Separation of Lift from Structure

The key design decision is the separation of lift from structure. The screw jack is treated purely as an axial lifting device, operating mainly in compression. Lateral forces and overturning moments — especially those from punching — are resisted by the surrounding telescopic column. This prevents side-loading of the screw jack, which would otherwise reduce efficiency, increase wear, and create binding risk. The final concept therefore behaves less like an exposed actuator and more like a guided structural mast.

Design Evolution

An earlier concept used two vertical linear rails at the rear with a lift carriage plate, with the screw jack near the centreline and a compliant interface for misalignment tolerance. That concept established the correct engineering principle but introduced too many precision-mounted components and tolerance sensitivity. Commercial off-the-shelf telescopic columns were also considered, but their side-load capability under boxing disturbances was uncertain. The project moved toward a custom telescopic column using SHS-based steel structure, Delrin bearing interfaces, and a modular 8080 inner member.

Fabrication Considerations

A light-shock service factor of approximately 1.5 was adopted because the robot may be adjusted in public demo environments with minor bumps and start-stop transients. Large SHS members were kept at ≥ 6 mm wall thickness, welded plates were sized to avoid distortion, and gusseted corner brackets were preferred where aluminium extrusion met steel structure.

Low-Level Control

The height adjustment is currently a motorised screw-jack system. The back-calculated motor speed requirement of approximately 800 rpm at the jack input drives the motor-sizing logic. The self-locking nature of the screw jack provides inherent fail-safe behaviour — the column holds its position even without motor power, ensuring safety during training.

Validation

The final height-adjustment concept satisfies the main functional requirements. The architecture provides the required 400 mm stroke, and the current motion target of approximately 32 seconds full stroke is consistent with the screw-jack input speed and motor-sizing logic.

The most important subsystem requirement — decoupling vertical lifting from lateral structural support — is satisfied by the final load path: vertical load passes through the screw-jack drive chain, while lateral punching loads are routed through the telescopic column into the outer welded structure and then into the base.

Current validation is strongest at the level of CAD packaging, stroke confirmation, concept selection logic, and load-path integrity. Remaining verification work includes: quantitative column deflection under lateral loading, repeated-cycle Delrin wear testing, and motor-control validation against the aspirational 10-second user preference.
