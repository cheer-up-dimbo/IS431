5.2 Robot Mechanism

The robot mechanism encompasses all physical hardware — mechanical structures, actuators, sensors, and supporting electrical and firmware systems — that together enable BoxBunny to behave as a realistic, interactive boxing training partner rather than a static target. At the system level, the mechanism has five principal responsibilities:

  1. Stable and safe base — resist punching-induced overturning moments, protect the user's footwork space, and remain portable for transport and public demonstrations.
  2. Rotation about yaw axis — re-angle the robot to present changing attack and defence lines during drills, replicating a coach's pivoting footwork.
  3. Height adjustability — match the robot's head and torso targets to users of different statures so that striking zones remain anatomically correct.
  4. Impact-absorbing contact surfaces — provide realistic, safe striking targets with embedded sensing for training analytics.
  5. Arm actuation — deliver targeted punches at anatomically correct striking zones using a high-velocity 2-DOF mechanism, enabling jabs, hooks, and uppercuts.

Note on Project Scope: While full footwork movement technically requires both linear (forward-backward) and rotational (yaw) movement, the project was scoped down to ensure high-quality execution within our timeframe. The mechanical development was branched into two main phases: Phase 1 handles static stability and the height adjustment subsystem, while Phase 2 focuses on the rotation subsystem. Consequently, linear movement has been scoped out of the current mechanical prototype.

These functions were decomposed from the broader project goals established in the problem clarification and design methodology phases. A key integration principle across all subsystems is that the mechanism was designed from system-level behaviour downward, rather than by combining isolated parts. The robot first had to remain upright, safe, and spatially compact in a boxing environment. Only after these high-level requirements were defined were they decomposed into subsystem-level functions following the left side of the V-model.

Overall Subsystem Architecture

Mechanical Stack

The mechanism is organised as a vertical stack, ordered from the ground up. This ordering principle ensures that the heaviest and most load-critical components sit closest to the base, where they can best resist overturning moments:

Interactive 3D Model: BoxBunny Mechanical Stack
  1. Trapezoidal Base : welded steel platform providing stability and portability
  2. Rotation Yaw Stage : low-profile rotation module at the stack base
  3. Telescopic Height-Adjustment Column : motorised screw-jack column with structural guiding
  4. Upper Body : torso, head, and 2-DOF coaxial striking arms with multi-layer padding

The rotational footwork axis is fully motorised for programmable, repeatable training sequences, allowing the robot to dynamically pivot and present changing attack lines. Meanwhile, the height adjustment uses a self-locking manual screw jack for safety and structural rigidity, ensuring the upper body remains stable under dynamic boxing loads.

Electrical Architecture

The BoxBunny robot employs seven distinct actuator and sensing subsystems distributed across three mechanical axes: base rotation, vertical height adjustment, and left and right arm actuation. Each axis is served by a dedicated motor, driver, and controller matched to its specific kinematic and safety requirements. The complete system is unified under a dual-rail power architecture that electrically isolates the high-current 24 V motor bus from the 12 V logic rail hosting the compute and control subsystems.

The four arm joints at the shoulder and elbow of each arm are driven by Damiao DM-J4310-2EC integrated brushless servo actuators, each rated at 24 V DC with a nominal output torque of 3 N·m and a peak of 7 N·m. An internal 10:1 planetary gearbox combined with a 3:1 external spur gear stage yields a 30:1 total reduction at the joint output shaft. Each actuator incorporates dual 14-bit magnetic absolute encoders, one on the motor side and one at the planetary output, enabling the internal digital signal processor to accumulate multi-turn position across the full operating range without requiring homing on power-up. The four actuators communicate with the Teensy 4.0 microcontroller over a shared CAN 2.0B bus at 1 Mbps; motor base identifiers are assigned as 0x01 to 0x04, and command frames are transmitted at identifiers 0x101 to 0x104 respectively.

The vertical height axis is driven by a CHP-36GP-555 brushed DC gear motor rated at 24 V with an integrated all-metal planetary gearbox at a 27:1 reduction ratio, yielding a no-load output speed of 440 RPM at the screw-jack input shaft. The motor is controlled by a Cytron MDDS10 dual H-bridge driver operating in Sign-Magnitude mode: a PWM signal on Pin 3 of the Teensy 4.0 sets motor speed via the AN1 terminal, while a digital direction signal on Pin 2 drives the DIG1 terminal. The HK2T screw-jack mechanism converts the rotary output to linear actuation, and the lead-angle geometry of the travelling nut provides inherent mechanical self-locking, ensuring the column holds position under load without requiring motor power. A RegenClamp V0.3 unit with a 10 Ω, 50 W wirewound brake resistor is interposed between the 24 V bus and the MDDS10 to dissipate regenerative back-EMF during hard-stop deceleration, limiting regen current to 2.65 A and protecting the power supply from overvoltage excursions.

The base rotation axis is driven by a Z55BLD400-24GU brushless DC motor rated at 400 W and 24 V, operating through a 5GU 26KB parallel-shaft gearbox in series with a 3.5:1 belt-and-pulley stage, for a combined system reduction ratio of 91:1. At the design operating point of 2275 RPM motor speed, the output platform rotates at 150 degrees per second, drawing approximately 33 W at steady state. Rotor position is measured by an AS5047P 14-bit SPI magnetic encoder mounted on the output shaft. The motor is driven by a ZBLD C20-800LRC CAN Modbus driver communicating at 125 kbps and commanded by an Arduino Uno R4 WiFi microcontroller. The Arduino receives target angle and speed instructions from the Jetson Orin NX over a WiFi UDP link and executes a 50 Hz PID control loop to track angular position references within a hard-coded operating envelope of ±90 degrees. A RegenClamp V0.3 unit is specified for this axis to manage regenerative energy during rapid direction reversals, with a 5 Ω, 100 W resistor recommended based on back-EMF analysis of the design operating condition.

Impact detection across the three foam padding zones is provided by four InvenSense MPU6050 six-axis inertial measurement units embedded within the padding structure. Each sensor communicates over I2C at 400 kHz. Because the MPU6050 supports only two addresses per bus (0x68 and 0x69, selectable via the AD0 pin), the Teensy 4.0's two independent hardware I2C peripherals are both utilised: the primary bus (Wire, Pins 18 and 19) carries the centre-body and left-body sensors, and the secondary bus (Wire1, Pins 17 and 16) carries the right-body and a reserved fourth sensor. All sensors are configured for a full-scale accelerometer range of ±16g, providing a maximum linear measurement of approximately 157 m/s² before saturation, which is sufficient for the peak impact accelerations expected during training operation.

Data Architecture

The system implements a two-tier hierarchical control architecture. The upper tier resides on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX compute module running ROS 2 Humble, which hosts the graphical control interface (unified_GUI_V4.py), performs combat finite-state-machine decision-making, sequences strike libraries, and processes impact detection events. The lower tier resides on a Teensy 4.0 microcontroller executing a deterministic 200 Hz unified control loop with a 5 ms period. Within each cycle, the Teensy performs CAN motor command transmission and feedback reception for the four arm actuators, PWM and direction signal output to the Cytron MDDS10 height driver, I2C polling of all four MPU6050 inertial sensors, and publication of a 21-element feedback payload to the Jetson via a micro-ROS USB bridge. The payload carries motor positions at indices 0 to 3, motor phase currents at indices 4 to 7, a CAN receive frame count at index 8, and three-axis acceleration readings for each of the four IMU sensors at indices 9 to 20.

The arm actuator control loop operates in Position-Speed mode. The Jetson publishes target joint positions and speed constraints at 100 Hz over the ROS 2 topic motor_commands. The Teensy receives these targets and transmits CAN command frames using a sparse edge-triggered protocol: a frame is transmitted only when a target position changes by more than 0.01 rad relative to the previously commanded value, or when a 100 ms keep-alive timeout expires. This strategy reduces average bus utilisation from approximately 80 percent under continuous polling to under 15 percent during typical sparring operation. The internal FOC controller within each Damiao actuator independently manages torque regulation and trajectory interpolation, closing the inner control loop at the actuator level without requiring explicit torque commands from the host.

A hardware-level current watchdog running at 200 Hz on the Teensy provides a last-resort safety layer for the arm motors. If any motor phase current exceeds 3.0 A, all four actuators are immediately disabled and their target positions are frozen at the last measured values. This watchdog operates independently of the GUI and cannot be suppressed by software commands, ensuring that mechanical failures or unexpected contact events do not result in uncontrolled motor motion.

The base rotation axis employs a dedicated angle-tracking PID controller running at 50 Hz on the Arduino Uno R4 WiFi. The controller receives commands via WiFi UDP as absolute or relative angle targets and peak RPM limits. At each control cycle, the angular error is computed as the difference between the target and the current AS5047P encoder reading. Proportional, integral, and derivative correction terms are summed and saturated to the commanded peak RPM ceiling before being dispatched to the ZBLD CAN Modbus driver. A ±1 degree dead zone prevents motor activation on residual encoder noise when the platform is at rest near the target. Hard position limits at ±90 degrees are enforced within the control law: when the encoder reading exceeds a limit in the direction of commanded motion, the output is zeroed and the target is clamped to the boundary; motion away from the limit is always permitted, enabling recovery from a boundary condition via any subsequent command. The inherent proportional nature of the PID output provides soft-start acceleration on large set-point changes, eliminating the discrete acceleration-ramp state machine used in earlier firmware revisions, which had been found to trigger power supply overcurrent protection at 1.58 A under abrupt speed commands.

The height axis operates under open-loop timed PWM control. The Jetson publishes ascend, descend, or stop commands via the ROS 2 topic /robot/height_cmd; the Teensy translates these into magnitude and direction signals for the MDDS10 driver. Although the CHP-36GP-555 motor is equipped with an AB-phase Hall encoder, this feedback channel is not utilised in the current implementation. Position tracking is managed by the operator through timed command sequences, with the self-locking screw-jack mechanism providing passive hold at any intermediate height without motor power.

Impact sensing operates continuously within the 200 Hz Teensy loop. Raw six-axis data from each MPU6050 is read via I2C in each cycle, converted to SI units using the ±16g accelerometer scale factor of 2048 LSB per g, and packed into the micro-ROS feedback payload. On the Jetson, the GUI processes all accumulated samples since the last display refresh, computing the L2 vector magnitude across the three acceleration axes for each sensor. When the magnitude of any sensor exceeds a configurable threshold, the event is classified as a valid strike and published to the ROS 2 topic /robot/strike_detected with the sensor identifier, peak acceleration value, and event timestamp.
