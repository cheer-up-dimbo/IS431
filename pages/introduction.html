IS-431: An Intelligent Boxing Robot to Assist in Boxing Training

Introduction & Project Background — Chapters 1, 2, and 4.1–4.2

1. Introduction

Boxing is widely recognised as a physically demanding and inherently dangerous sport. The World Medical Association has raised particular concern regarding the risk of chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), a degenerative brain condition linked to repeated head trauma sustained during training and competition (G., Magdalena, Lindsay, I., & M., 2019). Beyond long-term neurological risk, acute injuries are also prevalent at all levels of the sport. A comparative study analysing 7,712 athlete exposures across three consecutive Olympic Games demonstrated that injury risk in boxing shows "no difference by sex, weight category, or tournament round", indicating that the hazards of the sport affect athletes indiscriminately, regardless of experience or competitive context (Lystad, Alevras, Rudy, Soligard, & Engebretsen, 2021).

These risks are concentrated during sparring, which is simultaneously the most effective training modality and the most injurious. Repetitive sub-concussive trauma from frequent and intense sparring has been identified as a significant risk factor for neurological sequelae (Stiller et al., 2014), creating demand for training methods that replicate the intensity and realism of sparring within a safer environment.

The sports technology sector has grown considerably in response to this demand. The global market is projected to reach USD 55.9 billion by 2028, driven by increasing adoption of AI-assisted coaching and real-time performance analytics (Grand View Research, 2018). Falling hardware costs and advances in computer vision have also rendered robotics a practical option for physical training applications, a transition already observed in tennis-ball machines and batting-practice robots deployed in professional academies. The boxing equipment market specifically was valued at approximately USD 4.1 billion in 2023 and is projected to grow through the decade, driven in part by the rise of boxing-based fitness among non-competitive practitioners (Mordor Intelligence, 2023).

This project, BoxBunny, addresses this gap by developing a boxing training robot capable of delivering dynamic punch stimuli and adapting training difficulty without requiring a human sparring partner. The system targets recreational to competitive boxers in gym settings and is intended to function as a supplementary training tool that extends the quality of independent practice. It is not designed to replace professional sparring, but to serve as a safe, intelligent adjunct to conventional boxing training.

2. Problem Clarification

This chapter presents the research and analysis undertaken to define the problem space, identify user needs, evaluate existing solutions, and consolidate findings into a validated problem statement and value proposition. Primary and secondary research methods were employed in parallel, with findings synthesised through a Value Proposition Canvas framework.

2.1 Domain Overview and Trends

Boxing is one of the most widely practised combat sports globally, with participation growing across Southeast Asia, Europe, and North America. Within sports technology, data-driven training tools have moved from elite institutions into mainstream gyms. Products such as FightCamp (FightCamp, 2017) and Hykso (Hykso, 2018) demonstrate consumer appetite for punch tracking and performance feedback. This shift has raised user expectations: practitioners now expect quantifiable feedback and interactive training experiences that passive equipment such as heavy bags and focus pads cannot provide.

Several robotic boxing products have entered the market, including the Nexersys, the STRYK RXT-1 (STRYK, 2020), and the BotBoxer by SkyTechSport (BotBoxer, 2010). While these systems offer interactive strike targets, they remain predominantly reactive, responding only to the boxer's inputs rather than initiating attacks or adapting strategy based on real-time performance. This gap between existing robotic capability and boxing-specific application defines the opportunity that BoxBunny is designed to address.

2.2 Background

Sparring is central to boxing development, enabling athletes to practise timing, defence, and adaptability under realistic conditions. However, as noted by Stiller et al. (2014), repetitive sub-concussive trauma from frequent and intense sparring training constitutes a significant risk for neurological sequelae, creating demand for safer alternatives that replicate sparring's training benefits without compromising athlete well-being.

To better understand this problem, the project team engaged directly with the boxing community by joining boxing gym memberships and observing standard training flows first-hand. User interviews and surveys were subsequently conducted at six boxing gyms across Singapore, and three leading competitive products were reviewed in depth. These findings informed two Value Proposition Canvases capturing the distinct jobs, pains, and gains of boxers and training support stakeholders respectively.

2.3 Primary Research: User Interviews and Surveys

2.3.1 Methodology

User interviews were conducted at six boxing gyms in Singapore to obtain a broad range of practitioner perspectives. The research method was guided by the Interaction Design Foundation (IDF) empathise-phase framework, employing unstructured interviews to enable qualitative exploration of user thoughts and experiences (IxDF, 2016). Survey forms were distributed to practitioners not encountered in person, to supplement interview data with additional viewpoints. Respondents were classified into two stakeholder groups:

  • Boxing-Gym Boxers — individuals who train recreationally or competitively in a boxing gym environment.
  • Training Support Stakeholders — coaches, gym owners, and parents who plan, coordinate, and support boxer development.

Interview questions were tailored to each segment and refined into a concise guiding set. The full question set is documented in Appendix 4: User Interview Questions.

2.3.2 Findings: Boxing-Gym Boxers

Interviews yielded 50 boxer respondents. Coaching gaps emerged as the most pressing concern: 80% of respondents cited limited coaching attention in group classes (Figure 1). This is closely related to the second-most common challenge, where 76% of respondents reported difficulty finding reliable training partners at a comparable ability level. Injury risk was raised by 36% of respondents as a deterrent to frequent live sparring. A further 72% expressed frustration with slow skill progression, particularly when constrained by mixed-ability group settings that force experienced practitioners to repeat fundamental drills. Full data is provided in Appendix 5: User Interview Data.

2.3.3 Findings: Training Support Stakeholders

Twelve training support stakeholders (coaches, gym owners, and parents) were surveyed. Priorities shifted toward teaching methods and scalability. Pad work and mitt drills were ranked as the most important training format (Figure 2), as these methods are essential for delivering individualised feedback. However, current coach-to-boxer ratios prevent the consistent one-on-one engagement these methods require, creating a structural capacity gap in gym instruction.

2.5 Value Proposition Canvas

Two Value Proposition Canvases (VPCs) were constructed — one for Boxing-Gym Boxers (Figure 3) and one for Training Support Stakeholders (Figure 4) — translating user interview findings and competitor gaps into targeted product opportunities. The VPC framework maps customer jobs, pains, and gains against the proposed pain relievers and gain creators offered by the product.

Boxers demand flexibility, quantifiable progress, and the realism of sparring. Primary frustrations centre on coaching scarcity and inadequate partner matching: 80% reported limited coaching attention, 76% struggle with training partner availability, and 72% feel constrained by slow progress in mixed-ability settings.

Training support stakeholders prioritise safety, structured skill development, and operational scalability. Their primary challenge is delivering individualised attention: pad work and defensive drills rank highest in teaching priority, yet current coach-to-boxer ratios prevent the one-on-one engagement these methods require.

Together, the two canvases established a unified set of product attributes that balance boxer demands for realism and measurable progress against stakeholder requirements for safety, structure, and operational efficiency.

2.5.1 Problem of Interest

User interviews, competitor analysis, and the five design considerations collectively reveal a persistent gap: no existing training technology consolidates performance analytics, realistic sparring interaction, structured skill progression, adaptive fight intelligence, and a modular platform into a single, research-validated system. Competitor offerings each address a subset of user needs, and none demonstrate design decisions grounded in primary user research. This gap defines the problem that BoxBunny is designed to solve:

"How might we design an intelligent boxing training system that can dynamically and safely spar with users while assisting coaches in guiding, monitoring, and sustaining boxer development both during and beyond training sessions?"

2.5.2 Value Proposition Statement

Based on insights from interviews, surveys, and the analysis of existing solutions, BoxBunny directly addresses the challenges faced by both boxers and coaching staff. The system provides training variety, personalises interaction to individual user builds and performance levels, and introduces structured feedback pathways that passive training equipment cannot offer. The resulting value proposition is stated as follows:

"The boxing robot delivers a safe and dynamic training experience that adapts to each individual, offering the realism of training with a sparring partner and the guidance of a coach, while supporting users of all levels to train effectively, build confidence, and continuously improve."
Design Direction BoxBunny is conceived as a human-assistive sparring ecosystem — redefining how boxing skills are learnt, practised, and mastered by serving simultaneously as an adaptive training partner for athletes and a scalable coaching extension for instructors.

3. Concept Development: Product Requirements

With the problem space validated through primary and secondary research in Chapter 2, this chapter establishes the design specifications required to address it. The concept development process proceeded in two sequential stages: first, existing competitor features and their associated user feedback were evaluated to identify concrete design improvements; second, each improvement was translated into verifiable product needs that define what BoxBunny must deliver at the system level. This two-stage process ensures that every product requirement is traceable directly to a validated user pain point or market gap.

3.1 Design Gap Analysis: From Competitor Feedback to Design Improvements

The three selected competitors — Nexersys, STRYK RXT-1, and BotBoxer — were evaluated against the five design considerations identified in Section 2.4. For each consideration, the features and verified user complaints of existing products were reviewed, and a corresponding design improvement was formulated to close the identified gap. This analysis is summarised in Table 4.

Notably, user-generated reviews surfaced recurring complaints that are not captured by feature checklists alone: the absence of haptic feedback and a stationary base were cited as barriers to realism, and cost and bulk were identified as key adoption barriers. These qualitative signals directly shaped the design improvements proposed for BoxBunny.

Performance Analytics Strike pads with impact sensors, skill assessments, competitive leaderboards, and multi-user modes are available across Nexersys and BotBoxer. However, these metrics operate in isolation — no product integrates sensor data with visual tracking for composite analysis. Refined into a unified performance quantification system recording punch power, accuracy, and timing with visual analytics. Sensor data is synchronised with computer vision tracking, and results are surfaced on shared dashboards to motivate progress and support coach-level oversight at scale.

Computer vision-based opponent behaviour and reactive full-body movement are introduced to replicate the unpredictability of human sparring responses.

Modes exist as discrete programmes with no adaptive progression logic linking them. Adaptive Fight Intelligence BotBoxer provides an evading target that dodges punches in real time, and STRYK provides voice coaching cues. Neither product initiates attacks or adapts its strategy based on the user's accumulated performance history. Enhanced into a responsive counterpunching system that reacts dynamically to user attack patterns, mimicking the read-and-respond behaviour of a real sparring opponent. User reviews describe it as "extremely expensive" and "too bulky for gym use", limiting its addressable market to large, well-funded facilities. Designed for both gym and home deployment without compromising durability, and simplified into a scalable, cost-efficient structure to enable wider adoption.

4. Final Design

With the problem space and user requirements established in Chapter 2, this chapter consolidates findings into a system architecture (4.1) and a user journey (4.2). These define the top-level structure and interaction model that inform the subsystem-level conceptual design presented in Chapter 5.

4.1 System Overview

The BoxBunny robot is organised into three primary subsystems, each addressing a distinct set of functional requirements derived from the product needs mapping (Appendix 6):

  • User Interface — a touchscreen-based control layer providing visual feedback, session configuration, real-time coaching cues, and post-session performance dashboards.
  • Robot Mechanism — the integrated physical platform comprising five mechanical subcomponents: Base (stability and portability), Rotation (yaw tracking), Height Adjustment (user-adaptive positioning), Padding (impact sensing and contact surfaces), and Arm Actuation (punch execution via a 2-DOF coaxial differential joint). Together these subsystems position the robot and deliver safe, realistic striking interaction.
  • Robot Intelligence — the computer vision and AI layer enabling real-time user tracking, punch classification, and adaptive drill control. This layer enables the robot to respond to the boxer's movements and adjust training behaviour dynamically.

For a detailed mapping of how each derived product need relates to these subsystems, see Appendix 6: Product Needs to Subsystem Mapping.

4.2 User Journey

Figure 6 presents a swim-lane diagram illustrating how the three subsystems collaborate across the five phases of a structured training session. During the Setup phase, only the User Interface is active: the boxer logs in, completes a proficiency assessment, and selects training mode and parameters. At the Countdown phase, all three subsystems initialise concurrently — the Robot Intelligence layer ramps computer vision inference from 6 Hz to 30 Hz and resets lateral and depth tracking baselines, while the Robot Mechanism performs automatic height adjustment using person-detection bounding-box targeting.

During Active Training, the three subsystems operate in a continuous closed-loop: the Intelligence layer detects and classifies user actions at 30 Hz with IMU confirmation; the Mechanism responds with arm actuation and yaw tracking; and the Interface presents live feedback, a round timer, combo display, and AI-generated coaching tips. A Rest phase between rounds displays round-level statistics, followed by a Results phase in which the Interface presents post-session analytics and the Intelligence layer generates personalised AI coach analysis using a large language model.
